Theorem 3.9.5 (An intrinsic tangential differential on a rectifiable set)20072C3F5DC1

Theorem 3.9.5 (An intrinsic tangential differential on a rectifiable set).20072C3F5DC1 Let $M\subset U$ be measurable, countably $m$-rectifiable and locally finite in $\mathcal H^ m$ measure, and let $f:U\to \mathbb R^ k$ be locally Lipschitz. Differentiation on any countable $C^1$ decomposition defines a measurable linear map

\[ d^ Mf_ x:T_ xM\to \mathbb R^ k \quad \text{for }\mathcal H^ m\text{-almost every }x\in M, \]

independent of the decomposition up to a null set. Its norm is at most the local Lipschitz constant of $f$. Where the ambient derivative exists, $d^ Mf_ x=Df(x)|_{T_ xM}$ at the almost-everywhere points under consideration. For scalar $f$, its Euclidean dual is the tangential gradient $\nabla ^ Mf$.

Proof

Use Theorem 3.5.7 and Lemma 3.5.8 to partition $M$ into countably many measurable chart pieces modulo a null set. Lemma 3.9.3 defines the maps on each piece and bounds their norms. The derivative of a Lipschitz chart function is measurable as a limit of difference quotients. In a chart with $g=f\circ F$, the ambient representative is the matrix

\[ Dg\bigl ((DF)^*DF\bigr )^{-1}(DF)^*. \]

It restricts to the required map on the tangent plane and is zero on its orthogonal complement. The inverse chart metric is continuous, so this matrix field is measurable. Thus the resulting field is measurable on the pieces and on their countable union.

For two decompositions, compare every pair of patches by Proposition 3.9.4. The countable union of its exceptional sets is null. The maps therefore agree on one full-measure subset of $M$; tangent identification there is Theorem 3.6.6. If $f$ is ambient differentiable at such a point, its expansion along a $C^1$ chart immediately gives $D(f\circ F)=Df\circ DF$, proving the restriction formula. For a scalar target, the Euclidean inner product on the tangent plane identifies a linear functional with a unique vector. This proves the independence assertion used in Definition 3.3.2; it does not prove the coarea formula or its almost-everywhere fiber geometry. $\square$
