Proposition 16.14.7 (The diffuse approximation also converges in the real flat norm)2AAA27455AE0

Proposition 16.14.7 (The diffuse approximation also converges in the real flat norm).2AAA27455AE0 For the currents $T_ j$ of Proposition 16.14.6, the real-current flat norm of Definition 5.3.14 satisfies

\[ \mathcal F_{\mathbb R}(T_ j-T)\leq \frac{5\sqrt2}{j}. \]

Thus even flat convergence, fixed compact support, and uniform mass and boundary-mass bounds do not preserve real rectifiability without an additional hypothesis.

Proof

For $y\in [0,1]^2$, write $S_ y^{12}$ for the corresponding oriented square, and let $p_ j(y)$ be the center of its grid cell. Values on grid boundaries may be assigned arbitrarily. The displacement is at most $\delta _ j=\sqrt2/(2j)$. Translating $S_ y^{12}$ along the segment from $y$ to $p_ j(y)$ gives the homotopy decomposition of Proposition 5.3.13:

\[ S_{p_ j(y)}^{12}-S_ y^{12}=\partial R_ y+B_ y, \qquad \mathbf M(R_ y)\leq \delta _ j, \qquad \mathbf M(B_ y)\leq 4\delta _ j. \]

Here $R_ y$ is the image of the interval product with the square, and $B_ y$ is the image of the interval product with its boundary. The estimates are exactly Proposition 5.3.17 with Lipschitz constant one, square mass one and boundary mass four.

Define averaged currents by integrating their test-form evaluations over $y$. These evaluations are measurable (smooth on each cell apart from its boundary), and all supports lie in the fixed cube $Q$. The uniform mass bounds imply that the integrals define finite-mass currents $R$ and $B$, with masses at most $\delta _ j$ and $4\delta _ j$. Their boundary identity follows by evaluating $R$ on the exterior derivative of a test form and interchanging the finite integrals. The average of $S_{p_ j(y)}^{12}$ is the first-family Riemann sum, while the average of $S_ y^{12}$ is the first summand of $T$. Thus this family's difference is $\partial R+B$ and has real flat norm at most $5\delta _ j$. Repeat for the second family and add the decompositions. The total bound is $10\delta _ j=5\sqrt2/j$. No integer-coefficient filling or integer flat-norm assertion is involved. $\square$
