Example 3.4.8 (All directional derivatives need not give differentiability)DF295D29EE84

Example 3.4.8 (All directional derivatives need not give differentiability).DF295D29EE84 Define $f(0,0)=0$ and $f(x,y)=x^3/(x^2+y^2)$ away from the origin. Then $f$ is globally Lipschitz and every directional derivative at zero exists, but $f$ is not differentiable there.

Proof

Away from zero,

\[ f_ x=\frac{x^4+3x^2y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2},\qquad f_ y=\frac{-2x^3y}{(x^2+y^2)^2}. \]

These derivatives are bounded uniformly. Integrating the gradient on a segment gives a uniform Lipschitz bound if the segment avoids zero. If it passes through zero, split the segment and use continuity at zero, which follows from $|f(x,y)|\leq \sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. Homogeneity gives $D_ vf(0)=v_1^3/(v_1^2+v_2^2)$ for every nonzero direction $v$. The coordinate derivatives are 1 and 0, so a derivative would have to be $(h_1,h_2)\mapsto h_1$. At $v=(1,1)$ the directional derivative is $1/2$, not 1. The directional values fail the linearity hypothesis of Lemma 3.4.6. $\square$
