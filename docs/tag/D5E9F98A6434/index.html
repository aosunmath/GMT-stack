Theorem 4.7.4 (Finite-perimeter sets in an interval)D5E9F98A6434

Theorem 4.7.4 (Finite-perimeter sets in an interval).D5E9F98A6434 A measurable $E\subset I$ has finite perimeter in $I$ if and only if it agrees almost everywhere with a finite union of intervals relative to $I$. Choose the intervals with disjoint interiors and with no artificial common endpoint separating two occupied intervals. Then

\[ D\chi _ E=\sum _{\text{interior left endpoints }\ell }\delta _\ell -\sum _{\text{interior right endpoints }r}\delta _ r, \]

and $P(E;I)$ is the number of those interior endpoints. Endpoints at $a$ or $b$ make no contribution to relative perimeter. This is a direct one-dimensional consequence of the BV definition of perimeter in [Sim18]; the proof is included below.

Proof

Assume finite perimeter. The right-continuous representative of $\chi _ E$ takes only values zero and one: approach any point from the right through points where it agrees with the original characteristic function. The left limits also take only these values. Every nonzero jump consequently has size one in absolute value. There are finitely many such jumps, since their total absolute mass is at most $P(E;I)$.

Away from those finitely many points the representative is continuous: the two one-sided limits agree with its value. On each intervening interval, a continuous function with values in $\{ 0,1\} $ is constant. Thus $E$ is almost everywhere a finite union of occupied intervals. Its derivative there is exactly the displayed sum, by integration of a test derivative on each interval. The positive and negative endpoint atoms have distinct supports after the stated merging, so total variation counts them. Conversely, that same computation for any finite union produces a finite derivative measure and hence finite perimeter. In particular the empty set and the whole interval both have relative perimeter zero. $\square$
