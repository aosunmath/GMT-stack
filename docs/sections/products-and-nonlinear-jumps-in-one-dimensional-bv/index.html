4.8 Products and nonlinear jumps in one-dimensional BVAD92E313BC2A

AD92E313BC2A

Let $I=(a,b)$ be bounded. We use the right-continuous representatives and left limits from Theorem 4.7.2. The measure framework is that of [Sim18], Chapter 2, Section 2; the partition arguments below give explicit one-dimensional proofs of the calculus formulas. At a jump, evaluating an ordinary derivative at only one side generally gives the wrong coefficient.

Theorem 4.8.1 (The divided-difference chain rule on an interval).24962712EC88 Let $u\in BV(I)$ and $\Phi \in C^1(\mathbb R)$. Then $\Phi (u)\in BV(I)$ and

\[ D(\Phi (u))=K_\Phi (u^-,u^+)\, Du, \qquad K_\Phi (s,t)=\int _0^1\Phi '((1-\theta )s+\theta t)\, d\theta . \]

Equivalently $K_\Phi (s,t)=(\Phi (t)-\Phi (s))/(t-s)$ for $s\ne t$, and $K_\Phi (s,s)=\Phi '(s)$. In particular, at a jump point $x$ the new atom has mass $\Phi (u^+(x))-\Phi (u^-(x))$. Away from the countable jump set the coefficient equals $\Phi '(u^+(x))$. We prove this one-dimensional formula directly from the measure formulation of BV in [Sim18], including its jump and singular-continuous parts.

Proof

The regulated representative of $u$ is bounded. On an interval containing its range, $\Phi '$ is bounded; extend the restriction of $\Phi $ to a globally Lipschitz function. Proposition 4.4.6, together with boundedness of $I$ and the composition, gives $\Phi (u)\in BV(I)$. Its right-continuous representative is $\Phi (u^+)$. The integral defining $K_\Phi $ shows that it is continuous and bounded on the compact square containing all one-sided values.

Fix $a<s<t<b$ and take partitions $s=x_0<\cdots <x_ N=t$ with mesh tending to zero. Write $u_ i=u^+(x_ i)$ and $\mu =Du$. Telescoping and the interval representation give

\[ \Phi (u^+(t))-\Phi (u^+(s)) =\sum _ iK_\Phi (u_{i-1},u_ i)\mu ((x_{i-1},x_ i]). \]

Regard the coefficients as a bounded step function on $(s,t]$. For each fixed $r$ in that interval, the upper endpoint of its half-open cell tends to $r$ from the right or equals $r$, and the lower endpoint tends to $r$ strictly from the left. Therefore the corresponding values converge to $u^+(r)$ and $u^-(r)$. Continuity of $K_\Phi $ and dominated convergence with respect to the finite measure $|\mu |$ yield

\[ \Phi (u^+(t))-\Phi (u^+(s)) =\int _{(s,t]}K_\Phi (u^-,u^+)\, d\mu . \]

The left side is $D(\Phi (u))((s,t])$ by Theorem 4.7.2. Equality on these half-open intervals determines the finite signed measure on $I$, by exhaustion and the uniqueness of a measure on its interval generators. Multiplying the coefficient at an atom by $Du(\{ x\} )=u^+(x)-u^-(x)$ gives the asserted jump mass. $\square$

Proposition 4.8.2 (The midpoint product rule).AAACCD4144D2 For $u,v\in BV(I)$ define $\overline u=(u^-+u^+)/2$ and $\overline v=(v^-+v^+)/2$. Then $uv\in BV(I)$ and

\[ D(uv)=\overline u\, Dv+\overline v\, Du. \]

One also has the asymmetric formula $D(uv)=u^+Dv+v^-Du$; the symmetric formula makes the role of simultaneous jumps explicit.

Proof

Boundedness of the representatives implies

\[ \operatorname {Var}(u^+v^+;I) \leq \| u^+\| _\infty \operatorname {Var}(v^+;I) +\| v^+\| _\infty \operatorname {Var}(u^+;I). \]

Alternatively, membership in $BV$ follows from $uv=((u+v)^2-(u-v)^2)/4$ and Theorem 4.8.1. Apply that theorem to the squares: $D(w^2)=(w^-+w^+)Dw=2\overline w\, Dw$. Linearity of $D$ and of the midpoint representative gives the symmetric formula.

To compare the asymmetric formula, their difference is

\[ \tfrac 12(u^+-u^-)Dv-\tfrac 12(v^+-v^-)Du. \]

Both coefficient functions vanish off their countable jump sets. A nonatomic part of either finite measure gives zero mass to that countable union. At a point $x$, the atom of the first term is $\tfrac 12\Delta u(x)\Delta v(x)$, exactly the atom of the second. Thus the measures cancel. This verification includes common jump points and does not assume either function continuous. $\square$

Corollary 4.8.3 (Stieltjes integration by parts with traces).87F88951FED0 For $u,v\in BV(a,b)$,

\[ \int _{(a,b)}\overline u\, dDv+\int _{(a,b)}\overline v\, dDu =u(b-)v(b-)-u(a+)v(a+). \]

In particular, for $\phi \in C^1([a,b])$,

\[ \int _{(a,b)}\phi \, dDu+\int _ a^ bu\phi '\, dx =u(b-)\phi (b)-u(a+)\phi (a). \]
Proof

The total mass of $Dw$ for an interval BV function is $w(b-)-w(a+)$ by its cumulative representation. Apply this to $w=uv$ in Proposition 4.8.2. The bounded midpoint representatives are Borel, so both integrals against finite signed measures are well defined. For smooth $\phi $, its two one-sided representatives agree, $D\phi =\phi '\mathcal L^1$, and $\overline u=u$ almost everywhere for Lebesgue measure. This gives the second formula with the stated signs. $\square$

Example 4.8.4 (Squaring a singular-continuous function).691370FB4A4D Let $C$ be the Cantor function on $(0,1)$. Then

\[ D(C^2)=2C\, \mu _ C, \]

a singular-continuous positive measure of total mass one. In particular $\int C\, d\mu _ C=1/2$.

Proof

The function $C$ is continuous, with $DC=\mu _ C$ by Example 16.1.11. Theorem 4.8.1 therefore reduces to the coefficient $2C$ everywhere. The derivative is singular because it is supported on the Cantor set, and nonatomic because $\mu _ C$ is nonatomic. It is positive since $C\geq 0$. Its mass equals $C(1-)^2-C(0+)^2=1$ by the trace identity, yielding the integral. This also provides the variation of $C^2$ without replacing its derivative by the almost-everywhere classical derivative, which vanishes off the Cantor set. $\square$

Example 4.8.5 (Why a one-sided derivative is wrong at a jump).531B009B734F For $0<c<1$, set $u=\chi _{[c,1)}$ on $(0,1)$. Then $Du=\delta _ c$ and $D(u^ k)=\delta _ c$ for every integer $k\geq 1$. For $k=2$, the formula $2u^+Du$ would instead give $2\delta _ c$, and $2u^-Du$ would give zero. For $k=3$, evaluating the ordinary derivative at the midpoint would give $\tfrac 34\delta _ c$, also incorrect.

Proof

The function takes only the values zero and one, so $u^ k=u$. Its single jump is from zero to one. The chain coefficient for $\Phi (s)=s^ k$ is the divided difference $K_\Phi (0,1)=1$, as in Theorem 4.8.1. For a square this happens to equal $2(0+1)/2$, explaining the midpoint product rule. For a cube the value of $\Phi '$ at $1/2$ is $3/4$, whereas its average along the whole jump is $\int _0^13\theta ^2\, d\theta =1$. Thus the general nonlinear rule needs that average, not a single chosen value of the classical derivative. $\square$
