16.23 Mean values and model checks for graphical flow0FC8A463CC1C

0FC8A463CC1C

These examples test the conclusions of Sections 14.19 and 14.20. They use the graphical equation studied in [EH89], Section 3, but the periodic mean-value computations below are direct calculations.

Example 16.23.1 (Curve graphs preserve their ordinary spatial average).16419E95AD1A Let $u(x,t)$ be a smooth periodic curve graph with spatial period $\ell $. Then

\[ u_ t=\frac{u_{xx}}{1+u_ x^2}=\partial _ x\arctan u_ x, \qquad \frac d{dt}\int _0^\ell u(x,t)\, dx=0. \]

If the solution is smooth for all positive time, its limiting height is exactly $\ell ^{-1}\int _0^\ell u(x,0)dx$.

Proof

In dimension one Proposition 14.3.13 has the displayed coefficient. Differentiating $\arctan u_ x$ gives the divergence expression. Integration over the period has no endpoint term because $u_ x$ is periodic. If the flow is all-time smooth, Theorem 14.20.4 gives uniform convergence to a constant, so its conserved integral identifies that constant. This conservation law comes from the one-dimensional equation; it is not a consequence merely of the area being translation invariant in height. $\square$

Example 16.23.2 (A two-dimensional graph changes its spatial average).A90A63365A59 On the square torus with side length $2\pi $, set

\[ f(x,y)=\cos x+\cos y+\alpha \cos (x+y),\qquad u(x,y,0)=\delta f(x,y), \]

where $\alpha \ne 0$ is fixed. For any smooth graphical solution with this initial value, its normalized average $\bar u(t)$ satisfies

\[ \bar u'(0)=-\frac\alpha 2\delta ^3+O(\delta ^5) \quad \text{as }\delta \to 0. \]

Thus it is nonzero for sufficiently small nonzero $\delta $. The construction also gives examples in every dimension at least two by making the height independent of the extra variables.

Proof

All averages in this proof are normalized by $(2\pi )^{-2}$. At the initial time the equation gives

\[ \bar u'(0)=-\delta ^3\operatorname {avg} \frac{f_ i f_ j f_{ij}}{1+\delta ^2|Df|^2}, \]

with summation over $i,j\in \{ 1,2\} $, since the average of $\Delta f$ is zero. The bounded trigonometric derivatives justify replacing the denominator by one with an $O(\delta ^2)$ error inside this average.

Periodic integration by parts gives $\operatorname {avg}(f_ i f_ j f_{ij})=-\tfrac 12\operatorname {avg}(|Df|^2\Delta f)$. Writing $s=x+y$, one has

\[ |Df|^2=\sin ^2x+\sin ^2y+2\alpha ^2\sin ^2s +2\alpha \sin s(\sin x+\sin y), \quad \Delta f=-\cos x-\cos y-2\alpha \cos s. \]

Every product has zero average except $-2\alpha \sin s\sin x\cos y$ and $-2\alpha \sin s\sin y\cos x$. Each has average $-\alpha /2$, using $\sin (x+y)=\sin x\cos y+\cos x\sin y$ and the averages of squared sines and cosines. Therefore $\operatorname {avg}(|Df|^2\Delta f)=-\alpha $ and the contracted Hessian average is $\alpha /2$. This gives the formula and its nonvanishing conclusion. In higher dimensions the zero extra derivatives leave the same normalized calculation. This computes the prescribed initial velocity of any smooth solution; existence itself is a separate parabolic input. $\square$

Example 16.23.3 (A single Fourier mode is not an exact nonlinear flow).F41C1B96CB07 On a circle of length $2\pi $, the ansatz $u(x,t)=a(t)\cos x$ satisfies graphical mean curvature flow on a time interval only if $a$ vanishes identically. The linear heat ansatz $a(t)=a_0e^{-t}$ is an approximation at small amplitude, with exact residual

\[ u_ t-\frac{u_{xx}}{1+u_ x^2} =-\frac{a(t)^3\cos x\sin ^2x}{1+a(t)^2\sin ^2x}. \]

Its uniform residual is at most $|a(t)|^3$.

Proof

For the general ansatz the equation reads $a'\cos x=-a\cos x/(1+a^2\sin ^2x)$. At $x=0$ it requires $a'=-a$. At any point where $\cos x\ne 0$ and $\sin x\ne 0$, the same equation, together with $a'=-a$, forces $a=0$. Thus at every time in the interval the amplitude vanishes. For $a'=-a$ without requiring an exact solution, subtraction gives the stated residual. Its bound follows from $|\cos x|,|\sin x|\leq 1$ and the denominator being at least one. Exponential linear decay therefore does not furnish an exact nonlinear solution by itself. $\square$
