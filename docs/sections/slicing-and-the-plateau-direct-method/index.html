5.4 Slicing and the Plateau direct methodAB62CA3EC367

AB62CA3EC367

Restricting a current to a sublevel set creates a new boundary on the level set. Slicing identifies this boundary for almost every level and estimates its mass. The statements below use real-valued Lipschitz slicing functions and integer coefficients, following [Sim18], Chapter 6, Section 4, pp. 180–185.

Definition 5.4.1 (Slice by a scalar function).4F695BB95218 Let $T$ be a locally normal $m$-current, $m\geq 1$, and let $f:U\to \mathbb R$ be locally Lipschitz. Its lower slice, initially defined as a distributional current, is

\[ \langle T,f,t\rangle =\partial (T\llcorner \{ f<t\} )-(\partial T)\llcorner \{ f<t\} . \]

This formula alone does not give finite mass at every level. The almost-everywhere mass and rectifiability statements are the content of the slicing theorem. The boundary sign is fixed by restricting to $\{ f<t\} $; compare [Sim18], equations (4.6)–(4.7).

Theorem 5.4.2 (Slicing of integral currents).0E99E4802BCA Let $T=\llbracket M,\theta ,\tau \rrbracket $ be locally integral in $U$, and let $f:U\to \mathbb R$ be locally Lipschitz. For almost every $t$, the slice $\langle T,f,t\rangle $ is a locally integral $(m-1)$-current supported in $\operatorname {spt}T\cap f^{-1}(t)$; the restriction $T\llcorner \{ f<t\} $ is locally integral as well. For a Borel set $E\subset U$,

\[ \int _{\mathbb R}\| \langle T,f,t\rangle \| (E)\, dt =\int _ E|\nabla ^ M f|\, d\| T\| . \]

In particular, if $f$ is $L$-Lipschitz, this is at most $L\| T\| (E)$. On the rectifiable part of $M\cap f^{-1}(t)$ where $\nabla ^ M f\ne 0$, the slice carries multiplicity $\theta $ and orientation $\tau \mathbin {\lrcorner }(\nabla ^ M f/|\nabla ^ M f|)$, where $\langle \tau \mathbin {\lrcorner }v,\eta \rangle =\langle \tau ,v\wedge \eta \rangle $.

Sources: [Sim18], Definition 4.4 and Lemma 4.5, pp. 181–183. The rectifiable coarea formula gives the mass identity. Identifying this geometric slice with the distributional boundary in Definition 5.4.1, including a single exceptional null set for all test forms, is a further argument whose proof remains external. All conclusions concerning a slice are almost-everywhere in the level.

Proposition 5.4.3 (Boundary and slicing have opposite signs).90329BDB0E5A If $m\geq 2$, then at almost every level for a locally integral current,

\[ \partial \langle T,f,t\rangle =-\langle \partial T,f,t\rangle . \]

If $m=1$, the slice is a zero-current and its boundary is zero by convention. See [Sim18], Lemma 4.5(3).

Proof

Apply $\partial $ to the definition of the slice. The boundary-squared term vanishes, leaving $-\partial ((\partial T)\llcorner \{ f<t\} )$. Apply the same definition to $\partial T$ and use $\partial ^2T=0$ to identify it with the negative slice of $\partial T$. The almost-everywhere qualification ensures the finite-mass and integral conclusions for both slices. $\square$

Proposition 5.4.4 (Choosing a radius with controlled cutting boundary).73C4D018ADAF Let $T$ be locally integral near $\overline B_ R(a)$ and let $0<r<R$. For almost every $s\in (r,R)$,

\[ \partial (T\llcorner B_ s(a)) =(\partial T)\llcorner B_ s(a)+\langle T,|x-a|,s\rangle . \]

There is such an $s$ for which

\[ \mathbf M\bigl (\langle T,|x-a|,s\rangle \bigr ) \leq \frac{\| T\| (B_ R(a)\setminus \overline B_ r(a))}{R-r}. \]

Consequently the boundary mass of the restricted current is at most this quantity plus $\| \partial T\| (B_ R(a))$.

Proof

The distance function is one-Lipschitz. Apply the slicing theorem over the annulus and integrate over $s\in (r,R)$. The integral of the slice masses is no larger than the numerator, so some good level has mass no larger than their average. The restriction-boundary formula is the definition of the slice, and the boundary-mass estimate follows by the triangle inequality. A good level is chosen from the full-measure set where all slicing conclusions hold. $\square$

Example 5.4.5 (The slice of an oriented rectangle).A2CEDE0295D2 Let $T$ be integration over $[0,1]^2$ with orientation $e_1\wedge e_2$, and set $f(x,y)=x$. For $0<t<1$, $\langle T,f,t\rangle $ is the vertical segment $\{ t\} \times [0,1]$ oriented toward increasing $y$. Indeed $(e_1\wedge e_2)\mathbin {\lrcorner }e_1=e_2$. Its boundary is $\delta _{(t,1)}-\delta _{(t,0)}$. The slice of the oriented boundary of the rectangle has the opposite sign, directly illustrating Proposition 5.4.3.

Definition 5.4.6 (The integral Plateau problem in a compact container).959193546633 Fix $1\leq m<N$, a compact set $K\subset \mathbb R^ N$, and an integral $(m-1)$-current $Z$ of finite mass. The admissible class consists of integral $m$-currents $T$ with finite mass, $\operatorname {spt}T\subset K$, and $\partial T=Z$. The Plateau problem minimizes $\mathbf M(T)$ in this class. We assume the class is nonempty when asserting existence; in particular $\partial Z=0$ is necessary. No parametrization, connectedness, or genus is fixed by this definition. Compare the compactness approach in [Sim18], Chapter 6, Sections 3 and 7.

Theorem 5.4.7 (Existence of a mass-minimizing integral filling).5D2EEA517377 If the class in Definition 5.4.6 is nonempty, its mass infimum is attained by an integral current supported in $K$.

Proof

Choose one admissible current to bound the infimum from above, then take a minimizing sequence with bounded masses. Its boundary is always $Z$, so the boundary masses are uniformly bounded. Federer–Fleming compactness, Theorem 5.1.6, gives a weakly convergent subsequence with an integral limit supported in $K$. Boundary continuity gives $\partial T=Z$. Mass lower semicontinuity shows that its mass is no larger than the infimum, while admissibility gives the opposite inequality. Thus it attains the infimum. This is the direct-method consequence of the compactness theorem in [Sim18], Section 3; regularity of the minimizer is a separate problem. $\square$

Corollary 5.4.8 (Compact boundaries have Euclidean minimizers).2DDDF8908CF1 Let $1\leq m<N$, and let $Z$ be a compactly supported integral $(m-1)$-current of finite mass. If $m\geq 2$, assume $\partial Z=0$; if $m=1$, assume the total signed multiplicity of $Z$ is zero. Then there is a compactly supported integral $m$-current minimizing mass among all compactly supported integral fillings of $Z$ in $\mathbb R^ N$.

Proof

Choose a closed ball containing the support of $Z$. Proposition 5.3.19, or Example 5.3.20 when $m=1$, supplies a filling in that ball. The nearest-point projection $p$ to a closed Euclidean ball is one-Lipschitz and fixes $Z$. Every compactly supported integral filling $S$ therefore projects to another filling $p_\# S$ in the ball, with no larger mass, by Theorem 5.3.9. Thus the infimum in the ball is the full compactly supported infimum. Apply Theorem 5.4.7 in this fixed container. The use of compact supports makes properness automatic for the projection. $\square$
