12.8 The linearized operator of a single transitionF2423ADFBBD3

F2423ADFBBD3

We use $q(t)=\tanh (t/\sqrt2)$, $\psi =q'>0$, $V(t)=W''(q(t))=2-3\operatorname {sech}^2(t/\sqrt2)$ and

\[ \mathscr L=-\frac{d^2}{dt^2}+V(t),\qquad Q(f)=\int _{\mathbb R}(|f'|^2+Vf^2),\qquad \| \psi \| _2^2=c_ W=\frac{2\sqrt2}{3}. \]

The positive-gap and kernel questions are posed in [ChAC], Appendix B, Problem 2, pp. 28–29. We compute the sharp constant for this particular quartic potential. The ground-state identity of Proposition 12.6.2 is already available; the new point is coercivity after removing translation.

Lemma 12.8.1 (Two first-order factorizations).AFBF4999E313 Set

\[ \mathcal A=\frac d{dt}+\sqrt2\tanh (t/\sqrt2),\qquad \mathcal B=\frac d{dt}+\frac1{\sqrt2}\tanh (t/\sqrt2). \]

Their formal $L^2$ adjoints reverse the derivative sign, and on smooth functions

\[ \mathscr L=\mathcal A^*\mathcal A,\qquad \mathcal A\mathcal A^*=\mathcal B^*\mathcal B+\frac32, \qquad \mathcal B\mathcal B^*=-\frac{d^2}{dt^2}+\frac12. \]

For $f\in H^1(\mathbb R)$, $Q(f)=\| \mathcal A f\| _2^2$. Its zero space is exactly $\operatorname {span}\{ \psi \} $.

Proof

For $D=d/dt$ and a real function $b$, $(D+b)^*(D+b)=-D^2+b^2-b'$ and $(D+b)(D+b)^*=-D^2+b^2+b'$. Insert $b=\sqrt2\tanh (t/\sqrt2)$ and then $b=\tanh (t/\sqrt2)/\sqrt2$, using $\tanh ^2+\operatorname {sech}^2=1$. Integration by parts proves the quadratic identity for compactly supported smooth functions. Boundedness of the coefficients and density extend it to $H^1$.

Since $\psi '/\psi =-\sqrt2\tanh (t/\sqrt2)$, $\mathcal A\psi =0$. If $Q(f)=0$, the weak first-order equation gives $(f/\psi )'=0$ on every bounded interval, where $\psi $ is smooth and bounded away from zero. The constants agree on overlaps, so $f=c\psi $ globally. Conversely these functions lie in $H^1$ and have zero quadratic form. $\square$

Proposition 12.8.2 (The bounded kernel on the line).CF5564CA311E Every bounded $C^2$ solution of $\mathscr Lf=0$ on $\mathbb R$ is a constant multiple of $\psi $. More generally, all solutions have the form

\[ f(t)=\psi (t)\left(c_1+c_2\int _0^ t\psi (s)^{-2}ds\right). \]

The second independent solution grows exponentially as $t\to +\infty $ and is unbounded.

Proof

Write $f=\psi v$ and use $\mathscr L\psi =0$. The equation becomes $(\psi ^2v')'=0$, yielding the formula. Since $\psi (t)=2\sqrt2 e^{-\sqrt2t}(1+o(1))$ as $t\to +\infty $, its second solution is asymptotic to a nonzero constant times $e^{\sqrt2t}$. A bounded solution therefore has $c_2=0$. Conversely $\psi $ is bounded. This proves the one-dimensional assertion in [ChAC], Problem 2(b), without assuming that a bounded solution is square integrable in advance. $\square$

Lemma 12.8.3 (An adjoint primitive for compact orthogonal data).DF91D11FE50F If $f\in C_ c^\infty (\mathbb R)$ satisfies $\int f\psi =0$, then

\[ g(t)=-\frac1{\psi (t)}\int _{-\infty }^ t f(s)\psi (s)ds \]

is smooth and compactly supported, $\mathcal A^*g=f$, and

\[ Q(f)-\frac32\| f\| _2^2 =\| \mathcal B^*\mathcal B g\| _2^2+\frac32\| \mathcal B g\| _2^2. \]

Moreover $\| g\| _2^2\leq (2/3)\| f\| _2^2$.

Proof

The integral vanishes to the left of the support, and orthogonality makes it vanish to the right as well. Differentiate $\psi g$ to obtain $\mathcal A^*g=f$. Put $T=\mathcal A\mathcal A^*=\mathcal B^*\mathcal B+3/2$. Then $Q(f)=\| Tg\| _2^2$ and $\| f\| _2^2=\langle Tg,g\rangle $. Expand $T$ in these two expressions and integrate by parts; subtracting $3\langle Tg,g\rangle /2$ gives exactly the asserted sum. The lower bound $\langle Tg,g\rangle \geq (3/2)\| g\| _2^2$ gives the final estimate. All integrations here have compact support, so no endpoint behavior is assumed. $\square$

Theorem 12.8.4 (The exact gap away from translation).864856B2C443 If $f\in H^1(\mathbb R)$ and $\int f\psi =0$, then

\[ Q(f)\geq \frac32\| f\| _2^2. \]

Equality holds precisely for multiples of $\zeta (t)=\operatorname {sech}(t/\sqrt2)\tanh (t/\sqrt2)$. Thus $3/2$ is the optimal gap. For arbitrary $f\in H^1$,

\[ Q(f)\geq \frac32\left(\| f\| _2^2- \frac{|\int f\psi |^2}{c_ W}\right). \]

The existence of a positive gap is the assertion in [ChAC], Problem 2(c); the factorization determines its exact value here.

Proof

First apply Lemma 12.8.3 to compact orthogonal tests. For an arbitrary orthogonal $f$, approximate in $H^1$ by compact smooth functions and subtract a coefficient tending to zero times a fixed compact smooth function having nonzero pairing with $\psi $. This produces orthogonal $f_ j\to f$ in $H^1$, proving the inequality by continuity.

Let $g_ j$ be their adjoint primitives. Applied to differences, the last estimate of the lemma makes $g_ j$ Cauchy in $L^2$. Since $g_ j'=\sqrt2\tanh (t/\sqrt2)g_ j-f_ j$, the derivatives are also Cauchy in $L^2$. Thus $g_ j\to g$ in $H^1$ and $\mathcal A^*g=f$. If equality holds for $f$, the sum identity implies $\mathcal B g_ j\to 0$ in $L^2$, hence $\mathcal B g=0$. Solving this first-order equation gives $g=c\chi $, where $\chi =\operatorname {sech}(t/\sqrt2)$. Direct differentiation gives $\mathcal A^*\chi =(3/\sqrt2)\zeta $, so equality forces the stated form. Conversely $\mathcal B\chi =0$ and the factorization give $\mathscr L\zeta =(3/2)\zeta $. This function is in $H^1$ and is orthogonal to the even function $\psi $, so it realizes equality.

For general $f$, subtract its $L^2$ projection onto $\psi $. The quadratic form is unchanged because $\mathcal A\psi =0$, while the squared norm loses exactly the displayed projection term. This proves the last formula. $\square$

Proposition 12.8.5 (All square-integrable eigenfunctions of the normal operator).0D25EFCEBA69 If a nonzero real $f\in H^2(\mathbb R)$ satisfies $\mathscr Lf=\lambda f$, then either $\lambda =0$ and $f$ is a multiple of $\psi $, or $\lambda =3/2$ and $f$ is a multiple of $\zeta $. This classifies square-integrable eigenfunctions; it is not a statement about the full continuous spectrum.

Proof

The quadratic identity gives $\lambda \geq 0$. At zero the kernel was identified in Lemma 12.8.1. Suppose $\lambda >0$ and set $g=\mathcal A f$. It is nonzero, and intertwining yields $(\mathcal A\mathcal A^*)g=\lambda g$. Set $h=\mathcal B g$. The second factorization gives

\[ -h''+2h=\lambda h. \]

These operations are justified in $L^2$: the eigen-equation and bounded derivatives of $V$ imply successively that all derivatives of $f$ lie in $L^2$, starting from $f\in H^2$ and differentiating $f''=(V-\lambda )f$.

No nonzero solution of the displayed constant-coefficient equation belongs to $L^2(\mathbb R)$. For $\lambda <2$ its solutions are linear combinations of opposite exponentials, for $\lambda =2$ affine functions, and for $\lambda >2$ sines and cosines. Each nonzero combination fails square integrability at one or both ends. Hence $h=0$, so $g=c\chi $ and the nonzero equation for $g$ forces $\lambda =3/2$. Finally $\lambda f=\mathcal A^*g$ gives the stated eigenfunction. The converse eigen-equations have already been checked. $\square$

Theorem 12.8.6 (A normal inverse after fixing translation).3BF96C4734D7 For every $F\in L^2(\mathbb R)$ with $\int F\psi =0$, there is a unique $u\in H^2(\mathbb R)$ satisfying

\[ \mathscr Lu=F,\qquad \int u\psi =0. \]

With $\| u\| _{H^2}^2=\| u\| _2^2+\| u'\| _2^2+\| u''\| _2^2$, one has

\[ \| u\| _2\leq \frac23\| F\| _2,\qquad \| u\| _{H^2}\leq \sqrt7\| F\| _2. \]

Orthogonality of $F$ is necessary for any $H^2$ solution. This supplies an elementary normal-direction solvability statement for the linearized problem discussed in [ChAC], Problems 2 and 3; it is not a uniform inverse on a curved or noncompact interface.

Proof

On the closed subspace $\mathcal H=\{ u\in H^1:\int u\psi =0\} $, the gap and $V\geq -1$ give

\[ \| u\| _2^2\leq \frac23Q(u),\qquad \| u'\| _2^2\leq Q(u)+\| u\| _2^2\leq \frac53Q(u). \]

Thus $Q$ is an inner product with norm equivalent to the complete $H^1$ norm on this subspace. Hilbert representation, Lemma 5.7.1, applied to $v\mapsto \int Fv$, gives a unique $u\in \mathcal H$ with $Q(u,v)=\int Fv$ for every $v\in \mathcal H$. Both sides vanish on $\psi $, so decomposing a general $H^1$ test into its orthogonal part and its multiple of $\psi $ proves the equation weakly against all tests. Since $u''=Vu-F\in L^2$, $u\in H^2$.

Testing with $u$ gives $Q(u)\leq \| F\| _2\| u\| _2$ and hence the first estimate. It also gives $Q(u)\leq (2/3)\| F\| _2^2$, so $\| u'\| _2^2\leq (10/9)\| F\| _2^2$. Since $|V|\leq 2$, $\| u''\| _2\leq 2\| u\| _2+\| F\| _2\leq (7/3)\| F\| _2$. Adding the three squared estimates gives the constant seven. Uniqueness follows from the gap. Finally any $H^2$ solution paired with $\psi $ has $\int F\psi =Q(u,\psi )=0$, proving necessity. $\square$

Corollary 12.8.7 (The gap at a physical interface scale).FCE16961822E For $\varepsilon >0$, if $f\in H^1(\mathbb R)$ and $\int f(t)\psi (t/\varepsilon )dt=0$, then

\[ \int \left(\varepsilon |f'|^2+\varepsilon ^{-1}V(t/\varepsilon )f^2\right)dt \geq \frac3{2\varepsilon }\int f^2dt. \]

The operator $-\varepsilon ^2d^2/dt^2+V(t/\varepsilon )$ instead has gap $3/2$, while $-d^2/dt^2+\varepsilon ^{-2}V(t/\varepsilon )$ has gap $3/(2\varepsilon ^2)$. These are different normalizations of the same one-dimensional estimate.

Proof

Use $s=t/\varepsilon $ and apply Theorem 12.8.4 to $f(\varepsilon s)$. The energy on the left becomes its unscaled quadratic form, while its squared norm is $\varepsilon ^{-1}\int f^2$. Multiplying the resulting inequality by $\varepsilon $ or by $\varepsilon ^{-1}$ gives the other two normalizations. Equality is attained by the correspondingly rescaled function $\zeta $, so the constants remain sharp. $\square$
