16.1 Atomic, diffuse, and singular measures60DF2E469FE5

60DF2E469FE5

These examples make the distinctions between mass, support, density, and weak convergence explicit. The conventions are Definitions 2.1.1, 2.1.2, and 2.2.1. The foundational source is [Sim18], Chapter 1, Sections 1–5. The Cantor constructions below are proved directly; they illustrate the covering definitions rather than invoke a structure theorem for fractals.

Example 16.1.1 (A measure can be atomic and have an interval as support).A7AFF33C67FA Enumerate the rational points of $[0,1]$ without repetitions as $q_1,q_2,\ldots $, and set

\[ \mu =\sum _{j=1}^{\infty }2^{-j}\delta _{q_ j}. \]

This is a Radon probability measure on $\mathbb R$, concentrated on a countable set, with support $[0,1]$. At each $q_ j$, its one-dimensional density is infinite. In contrast, counting measure on $\mathbb Q\cap [0,1]$ is not locally finite.

Proof

Countable additivity follows by interchanging nonnegative sums. Finite partial sums are finite Radon measures, and their omitted tails have total mass tending to zero, which proves inner and outer regularity of $\mu $ by approximation. Every neighborhood of a point of $[0,1]$ contains some $q_ j$ and hence positive mass; outside that interval there are neighborhoods of zero mass. At $q_ j$, $\mu (B_ r(q_ j))/(2r)\geq 2^{-j}/(2r)\to \infty $. Counting measure assigns infinite mass to the compact interval $[0,1]$. Thus support alone records neither atomicity nor local finiteness. $\square$

Example 16.1.2 (Concentration of absolutely continuous measures).315BC1BE84A5 Let $\rho \geq 0$ belong to $L^1(\mathbb R^ n)$ with $\int \rho =1$, fix $a\in \mathbb R^ n$, and let $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$. Then

\[ d\mu _\varepsilon (x)=\varepsilon ^{-n}\rho ((x-a)/\varepsilon )\, dx \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \mu _\varepsilon \rightharpoonup \delta _ a. \]

Every approximating measure is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, while the limit is singular and atomic.

Proof

For $\varphi \in C_ c(\mathbb R^ n)$, changing variables gives $\int \varphi \, d\mu _\varepsilon =\int \varphi (a+\varepsilon y)\rho (y)\, dy$. The integrand converges pointwise to $\varphi (a)\rho (y)$ and is bounded by $\| \varphi \| _\infty \rho (y)$. Dominated convergence proves the claim. Local finiteness and regularity of the approximating measures follow from integration of a locally integrable density against Lebesgue measure. $\square$

Example 16.1.3 (Mass escapes through infinity or a domain boundary).72CA99D2039A In $\mathbb R$, $\delta _ j\rightharpoonup 0$ although each measure has total mass one. In the open domain $U=(0,1)$, $\delta _{1/j}\rightharpoonup 0$ for $j\geq 2$, whereas the same measures viewed on $\mathbb R$ converge to $\delta _0$.

Proof

A compactly supported continuous test vanishes at $j$ for all sufficiently large $j$. A compact subset of $(0,1)$ has positive distance from zero, so a test supported there also eventually vanishes at $1/j$. On $\mathbb R$, continuity instead gives $\varphi (1/j)\to \varphi (0)$. The choice of the ambient open set is part of Definition 2.1.2; preservation of total mass does not follow from that topology. $\square$

Example 16.1.4 (Weak convergence does not imply convergence on every Borel set).1CE8BCF4FD05 The sequence $\delta _{1/j}\rightharpoonup \delta _0$ on $\mathbb R$ has

\[ \delta _{1/j}(\{ 0\} )=0,\qquad \delta _0(\{ 0\} )=1, \qquad \delta _{1/j}((0,1))=1\quad (j\geq 2),\qquad \delta _0((0,1))=0. \]

Thus both a closed set and an open set can have discontinuous masses. The open-set lower-semicontinuity inequality in Proposition 2.1.4 remains valid.

Proof

Evaluation of a Dirac measure at a set is its indicator function at the atom. The four values follow immediately. Here zero lies on the boundary of each of the sets at issue and carries positive limiting mass; indicators of these sets are not continuous tests at that point. $\square$

Example 16.1.5 (Oscillating densities can average without converging in norm).60C0FD0D3245 On $[0,1]$, put $f_ j(x)=1+\sin (2\pi jx)$ and $d\mu _ j=f_ j\, dx$. Then $\mu _ j$ are probability measures and converge weakly on $\mathbb R$ to $\mathcal L^1\llcorner [0,1]$, but

\[ \int _0^1|f_ j-1|\, dx=\frac2\pi \]

for every positive integer $j$. In particular weak convergence here does not imply convergence of densities in $L^1$.

Proof

Nonnegativity and mass one follow by direct integration. For continuous $\varphi $ on $[0,1]$, partition into the $j$ intervals $I_ k=[k/j,(k+1)/j]$. The sine has zero integral on each. Subtracting $\varphi (k/j)$ on $I_ k$ bounds the oscillating integral in absolute value by the modulus of continuity of $\varphi $ at $1/j$, which tends to zero. The displayed norm is $j$ equal copies of one period, rescaled to total length one; the integral of $|\sin |$ over a period is four. $\square$

Definition 16.1.6 (The middle-thirds Cantor set and its probability measure).9D0DB6B0C777 Let $C_ k$ be the union of the $2^ k$ closed intervals of length $3^{-k}$ obtained by retaining the left and right thirds at each of $k$ steps, and put $C=\bigcap _{k\geq 0}C_ k$. For $t\in [0,1)$ write its binary digits as $b_ j(t)\in \{ 0,1\} $, choosing the expansion eventually zero at dyadic rationals. Define

\[ T(t)=\sum _{j=1}^{\infty }\frac{2b_ j(t)}{3^ j}, \qquad \mu _ C=T_\# (\mathcal L^1\llcorner [0,1)). \]

The pushforward means $\mu _ C(A)=\mathcal L^1(T^{-1}(A))$ for Borel sets $A$. Each finite digit function is measurable, so the limit $T$ is measurable. This constructs the measure without presupposing a measure on an infinite product space.

Proposition 16.1.7 (Cylinder masses and singular continuity).DCBE26BC98C4 The set $C$ is compact and has Lebesgue measure zero. The measure $\mu _ C$ is a Radon probability measure supported on $C$, and every level-$k$ retained interval $I$ has mass $2^{-k}$. It has no atoms. Thus a measure may be singular with respect to Lebesgue measure without being atomic.

Proof

The sets $C_ k$ are nested compact sets with total length $(2/3)^ k$, so their intersection is compact and null. The ternary series defining $T$ belongs to every $C_ k$. Specifying a word of $k$ binary digits selects a half-open dyadic interval of Lebesgue measure $2^{-k}$ and the corresponding retained ternary interval; endpoint conventions affect at most a countable null set. This proves the cylinder mass. Every point of $C$ lies in a retained interval at every level; its singleton mass is therefore at most $2^{-k}$ for all $k$. These same shrinking intervals show that every neighborhood of every point of $C$ has positive mass, so the support is exactly $C$. A finite Borel measure on $\mathbb R$ is regular ([Sim18], Chapter 1, Section 1); hence this probability measure is Radon. Alternatively, regularity follows by compact cylinder approximations and finite total mass. $\square$

Proposition 16.1.8 (Power-law bounds for the Cantor measure).4C394463E8E0 Write $s=\log 2/\log 3$. For every $x\in C$ and $0<r<1$,

\[ \frac18r^ s\leq \mu _ C(B_ r(x))\leq 8r^ s. \]

In particular $\mu _ C(B_ r(x))/(2r)\to \infty $ at every $x\in C$, despite the absence of atoms.

Proof

Choose $k\geq 1$ with $3^{-k}\leq r<3^{-k+1}$. The level-$k+2$ interval containing $x$ has diameter strictly less than $r$ and hence lies in $B_ r(x)$. Its mass is $2^{-k-2}$. Since $r^ s<2\cdot 2^{-k}$, this is at least $r^ s/8$. Distinct level-$k$ intervals have length $L=3^{-k}$ and gaps at least $L$. An interval of length $2r<6L$ meets at most eight of them. Their total mass is at most $8\cdot 2^{-k}\leq 8r^ s$. Finally $s<1$, so the lower bound divided by $2r$ diverges. The constants are deliberately nonsharp. $\square$

Definition 16.1.9 (Hausdorff dimension via diameter covers).7E12771384C9 For a metric subset $E$ and $q>0$, let

\[ \mathcal H^ q_{\rm diam}(E)=\lim _{\delta \downarrow 0} \inf \left\{ \sum _ i(\operatorname {diam}E_ i)^ q: E\subset \bigcup _ iE_ i,\ \operatorname {diam}E_ i<\delta \right\} . \]

Define $\dim _ H E=\inf \{ q>0:\mathcal H^ q_{\rm diam}(E)=0\} $. For integer $q=m\geq 1$, this measure differs from Definition 2.2.1 by the positive constant $2^ m/\omega _ m$; this constant does not affect dimension. The covering construction for real exponents is the one in [Sim18], Chapter 1, Section 2.

Proposition 16.1.10 (The Cantor set has a noninteger dimension).E16EF71E7152 For $s=\log 2/\log 3$,

\[ 0<\mathcal H^ s_{\rm diam}(C)<\infty ,\qquad \dim _ H C=s. \]

Thus zero length does not imply zero Hausdorff dimension.

Proof

The level-$k$ cover has sum $2^ k(3^{-k})^ s=1$, and for $q>s$ its $q$-sum tends to zero. For the lower bound take any sufficiently fine countable cover. A set of diameter $d>0$ meeting $C$ is contained, on $C$, in the ball of radius $2d$ about any chosen point of its intersection. Proposition 16.1.8 bounds its $\mu _ C$ outer measure by $8(2d)^ s$. Sets of diameter zero carry no mass. Countable subadditivity gives $1\leq 8\cdot 2^ s\sum _ i d_ i^ s$, hence a uniform positive lower bound. If $0<q<s$ and $d_ i<\delta $, then $\sum _ i d_ i^ q\geq \delta ^{q-s}\sum _ i d_ i^ s$, which tends to infinity as $\delta \downarrow 0$. This also verifies the dimension assertion directly. $\square$

Example 16.1.11 (The Cantor function has a singular distributional derivative).2C0749FA806B Let $F(x)=\mu _ C(( -\infty ,x])$. Then $F$ is continuous, nondecreasing, equals zero for $x\leq 0$ and one for $x\geq 1$, and is constant on every component of $\mathbb R\setminus C$. It satisfies

\[ F'=0\quad \mathcal L^1\text{-almost everywhere},\qquad DF=\mu _ C \]

as distributions. In particular it is locally BV but is not absolutely continuous on $[0,1]$.

Proof

A distribution function jumps precisely by its singleton mass, so Proposition 16.1.7 gives continuity. An interval disjoint from the support has zero mass, giving constancy there and zero classical derivative off the null set $C$. For $\varphi \in C_ c^\infty (\mathbb R)$, Fubini yields

\[ -\int F(x)\varphi '(x)\, dx =-\int _ C\int _ t^\infty \varphi '(x)\, dx\, d\mu _ C(t) =\int \varphi (t)\, d\mu _ C(t). \]

The finite measure derivative proves local BV. Absolute continuity on $[0,1]$ would imply $F(1)-F(0)=\int _0^1F'=0$, contradicting the endpoint values. This is an explicit singular continuous part of a BV derivative, distinct from the jump part of a step function. $\square$

Example 16.1.12 (Diffuse approximations to a singular continuous measure).26512807DFDC Let $C_ k$ be the level-$k$ Cantor approximation and define

\[ d\nu _ k=(3/2)^ k\mathbf1_{C_ k}\, dx. \]

Then $\nu _ k$ is an absolutely continuous probability measure and $\nu _ k\rightharpoonup \mu _ C$. Each retained interval has the same mass under $\nu _ k$ and $\mu _ C$, although their behavior inside it is different.

Proof

The total length of $C_ k$ is $(2/3)^ k$. Multiplying by $(3/2)^ k$ gives mass one, and each interval has mass $2^{-k}$. For a continuous test $\varphi $ on $[0,1]$, select one point $x_ I$ in each level-$k$ interval. The difference between either measure's integral and $\sum _ I2^{-k}\varphi (x_ I)$ is bounded by the modulus of continuity of $\varphi $ at $3^{-k}$. These two errors tend to zero, proving weak convergence. Thus concentration can produce a singular continuous limit as well as the Dirac limit in Example 16.1.2. $\square$

Example 16.1.13 (Three mutually singular components in one measure).8C2551B95ECF The finite measure

\[ \eta =\mathcal L^1\llcorner [0,1]+\mu _ C+\delta _0 \]

has an absolutely continuous part, a singular continuous part, and an atomic part. These three summands are mutually singular even though their supports overlap. Its distribution function on $\mathbb R$ is the sum of the distribution functions of the three summands.

Proof

The three measures are respectively concentrated on the disjoint Borel sets $[0,1]\setminus C$, $C\setminus \{ 0\} $, and $\{ 0\} $. Removing $C$ does not change Lebesgue measure, and removing zero does not change $\mu _ C$ because it has no atoms. Each summand is therefore singular to the others, while the first two supports are $[0,1]$ and $C$. Additivity gives the distribution-function assertion. This explicit decomposition does not require the general Lebesgue decomposition theorem. $\square$
