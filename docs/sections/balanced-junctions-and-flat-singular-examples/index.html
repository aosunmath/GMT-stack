6.11 Balanced junctions and flat singular examplesA976DD1EBE14

A976DD1EBE14

One-dimensional cones provide explicit singular models, and products turn them into higher-dimensional junctions. We expand the examples following [Minter], Lemma 1.5, pp. 15–16, and distinguish flat singular points from regular points of high multiplicity.

Definition 6.11.1 (A finite weighted ray cone).479485EBDA40 For distinct unit vectors $v_1,\ldots ,v_ k\in S^{N-1}$ and positive integers $q_1,\ldots ,q_ k$, define

\[ C=\sum _{i=1}^ k q_ i|R_{v_ i}|,\qquad R_{v_ i}=\{ t v_ i:t\geq 0\} . \]

Each ray records its line $\mathbb R v_ i$ and length measure. The vertex itself has zero weight. This is an integral $1$-cone; stationarity imposes the additional balance condition below. See [Minter], p. 15.

Proposition 6.11.2 (First variation and density of a ray junction).F9B23C2936BA For the cone of Definition 6.11.1,

\[ \delta C(X)=-X(0)\cdot \sum _{i=1}^ kq_ iv_ i. \]

Thus $C$ is stationary if and only if $\sum _ iq_ iv_ i=0$. Its vertex density is $\frac12\sum _ iq_ i$, and its density at a nonzero point of $R_{v_ i}$ is $q_ i$.

Proof

On one ray, tangential divergence is $\frac d{dt}(X(tv_ i)\cdot v_ i)$. Integration from zero to infinity gives $-X(0)\cdot v_ i$ by compact support. Arbitrary values of $X(0)$ prove the equivalence. The centered ball has mass $r\sum _ iq_ i$ and $\omega _1=2$. Near a nonzero point of one ray, all other finitely many rays are absent, so the local density is its multiplicity. $\square$

Lemma 6.11.3 (Polar measure of a one-dimensional cone).D981C02BEF95 If $C$ is a stationary $1$-cone on $\mathbb R^ N$ with weight $\mu $, there is a finite measure $\sigma $ on $S^{N-1}$ such that

\[ \int \phi (x)\, d\mu (x)=\int _{S^{N-1}}\int _0^\infty \phi (tv)\, dt\, d\sigma (v) \]

for every nonnegative Borel function $\phi $. Moreover $\Theta ^1(\mu ,v)=\sigma (\{ v\} )$ for every unit vector $v$.

Proof

There is no atom at zero by Corollary 6.6.4. Define $\sigma (A)=\mu (\{ tv:0<t<1,\ v\in A\} )$ for Borel $A\subset S^{N-1}$. Homogeneity gives the corresponding truncated-sector mass $r\sigma (A)$ for every $r>0$. Centered spheres have zero mass because $\mu (B_ r(0))$ is a constant times $r$. Subtracting truncated sectors proves that the mass of a polar rectangle $(a,b)\times A$ is $(b-a)\sigma (A)$. Uniqueness of measures on rectangles proves the formula.

For $0<r<1/2$, the part of the ray through $v$ inside $B_ r(v)$ has length $2r$. Conversely, if $tu\in B_ r(v)$, then $1-r<t<1+r$ and $|u-v|<2r$. Hence

\[ 2r\sigma (\{ v\} )\leq \mu (B_ r(v)) \leq 2r\sigma (\{ u:|u-v|<2r\} ). \]

Divide by $2r$ and let $r\downarrow 0$; finiteness of $\sigma $ gives the claimed density. $\square$

Theorem 6.11.4 (Classification of stationary integral one-dimensional cones).BC1EE471EB15 Every nonzero stationary integral $1$-cone in $\mathbb R^ N$, $N\geq 2$, is a finite weighted ray cone in the sense of Definition 6.11.1, with $\sum _ iq_ iv_ i=0$. In particular its density at the vertex is a positive half-integer. This is the one-dimensional assertion in [Minter], p. 15.

Proof

Use the polar measure $\sigma $ from Lemma 6.11.3. Density is constant along each ray by Proposition 6.9.1. Integrality makes the density a positive integer at $\mu $-almost every point. The polar formula and the lemma therefore show that $\sigma (\{ v\} )$ is a positive integer for $\sigma $-almost every $v$. Thus $\sigma $ is supported on atoms of integer mass at least one. Its finiteness implies that only finitely many such atoms occur, say $\sigma =\sum _ iq_ i\delta _{v_ i}$.

The polar formula identifies the weight of $C$ with that of the corresponding weighted rays. Both are rectifiable; Lemma 6.7.2 identifies the full varifolds. Proposition 6.11.2 gives the balance condition and density formula. The balance condition rules out a total weight of one; total weight two consists of two opposite unit-weight rays and is a line. $\square$

Corollary 6.11.5 (Density of cones with a codimension-one spine).5872374C9915 If a nonzero stationary integral $m$-cone has spine dimension exactly $m-1$, then its density at the vertex belongs to

\[ \{ 3/2,2,5/2,3,\ldots \} . \]

It is a finite union, with positive integer multiplicities, of $m$-dimensional half-planes meeting along the spine and satisfying the transverse ray balance.

Proof

For $m>1$, Theorem 6.3.8 expresses the cone as the product of its spine and a stationary integral $1$-cone. For $m=1$ use the cone itself. Apply Theorem 6.11.4. Each product ray is an $m$-dimensional half-plane and contributes $q_ i\omega _ mr^ m/2$ to a centered ball, so the density is $\sum _ iq_ i/2$. Total weight two would give a full $m$-plane with spine dimension $m$, contrary to the hypothesis; total weight one is unbalanced. Thus the total weight is at least three. $\square$

Example 6.11.6 (The triple junction and its products).9A121EC9BA8B Three unit-multiplicity planar rays with angles $120$ degrees form a stationary integral $1$-cone $Y$ of density $3/2$. For $m\geq 1$, $Y\times \mathbb R^{m-1}$ is a stationary integral $m$-cone in $\mathbb R^{m+1}$. Its spine and singular set are both $\{ 0\} \times \mathbb R^{m-1}$, and its density there is $3/2$.

Proof

The three unit direction vectors sum to zero, so Proposition 6.11.2 gives stationarity. Product integration preserves stationarity: the transverse divergence integrates to zero by stationarity of $Y$, while coordinate derivatives along the Euclidean factor integrate to zero by compact support. Away from the common axis the support is a single smooth multiplicity-one half-plane interior. On the axis three half-planes meet and the support is not a smooth manifold. Their three half-ball masses give density $3/2$ there and density one elsewhere. Proposition 6.9.4 identifies the axis as the spine. All tangent cones at axis points equal this same cone, so these points lie in $\mathcal S^{m-1}$ and, for $m\geq 2$, in no lower stratum. $\square$

Example 6.11.7 (A nonplanar cone with a smooth disconnected link).86409CB74347 In $\mathbb R^4=\mathbb R^2\times \mathbb R^2$, let $P=\mathbb R^2\times \{ 0\} $ and $Q=\{ 0\} \times \mathbb R^2$. The cone $C=|P|+|Q|$ is stationary and integral, has spine $\{ 0\} $, and has density two at zero. Its link is two disjoint great circles in $S^3$, with no junction points.

Proof

Both planes are stationary and meet only at zero. Density is one at nonzero support points and two at zero, so Proposition 6.9.4 gives the asserted spine. Their intersections with $S^3$ are disjoint smooth great circles. Thus nonplanarity of a two-dimensional cone does not, in arbitrary codimension, force a junction in its link. This example specifies the limitation of arguments based only on the presence of junctions. $\square$

Example 6.11.8 (A flat singular point from two smooth minimal graphs).67A305ACFB42 Let $\Omega =(-\pi /2,\pi /2)^2$ and

\[ u(x,y)=\log (\cos y)-\log (\cos x). \]

In $\Omega \times \mathbb R$, the sum of the varifolds of the graph of $u$ and the plane $z=0$ is stationary and integral. The origin is a flat singular point with density two and a unique tangent cone equal to twice the horizontal plane.

Proof

Here $u_ x=\tan x$, $u_ y=-\tan y$, $u_{xx}=\sec ^2x$, $u_{yy}=-\sec ^2y$, and $u_{xy}=0$. The minimal graph equation in two variables is

\[ (1+u_ y^2)u_{xx}-2u_ xu_ yu_{xy}+(1+u_ x^2)u_{yy}=0, \]

which these derivatives satisfy. Thus both smooth graphs are stationary for compactly supported variations in the indicated open cylinder. At zero, $u=0$ and $Du=0$, so the tangent cone of their sum is twice the horizontal plane; smoothness makes this blow-up unique.

The support is not a single smooth surface near zero. If it were, its horizontal tangent plane would make it a graph on a smaller neighborhood by the inverse function theorem. But the support contains both the zero graph and nonzero values of $u$ arbitrarily close to zero, giving two heights over some base points. This contradiction proves singularity. The graphs cross along $y=\pm x$; this is not one-sided contact prohibited by the strong maximum principle. It also differs from a regular multiplicity-two plane, which has only one geometric sheet as a set. $\square$
