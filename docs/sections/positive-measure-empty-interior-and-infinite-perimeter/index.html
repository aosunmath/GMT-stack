16.11 Positive measure, empty interior and infinite perimeterF473CA4D4F14

F473CA4D4F14

A positive-length Cantor set separates measure from topology and rectifiability from finite boundary mass. The construction is in [Mor16], fifth edition, Example after Proposition 3.12, p. 33; the associated current is Exercise 4.17, p. 59. We give the construction and the variation argument explicitly.

Definition 16.11.1 (A fat Cantor construction).2F0A87D1DCB7 Start with $E_0=[0,1]$. At step $n\ge 1$, remove the central open interval of length $4^{-n}$ from each of the $2^{n-1}$ closed components of $E_{n-1}$. Let $E_ n$ be the remaining union of $2^ n$ closed intervals and define $E=\bigcap _{n\ge 0}E_ n$.

Proposition 16.11.2 (Length and topology of the fat Cantor set).51F772C179A3 The construction in Definition 16.11.1 is well defined. Every stage-$n$ component has length

\[ \ell _ n=2^{-n-1}+2^{-2n-1}, \]

and its intersection with $E$ has length $2^{-n-1}$. The set $E$ is compact, perfect, nowhere dense, and has Lebesgue measure $1/2$.

Proof

The recurrence $\ell _ n=(\ell _{n-1}-4^{-n})/2$, with $\ell _0=1$, gives the displayed formula by induction. It is positive and the removed length is smaller than its parent interval, validating every step. At step $n$ the removed total length is $2^{n-1}4^{-n}=2^{-n-1}$. These intervals are disjoint, so their total length is $1/2$, proving $|E|=1/2$ by continuity from above. Within a fixed stage-$n$ interval, the total future removed length is

\[ \sum _{j\ge 1}2^{j-1}4^{-(n+j)}=\tfrac 12 4^{-n}. \]

Subtracting it from $\ell _ n$ gives $2^{-n-1}$. Compactness follows by nested closed intersections. Since $\ell _ n\to 0$, no interval can be contained in every $E_ n$, so $E$ has empty interior and, being closed, is nowhere dense. Endpoints of each component survive every later step. For any $x\in E$, choose a stage-$n$ component containing it and an endpoint different from $x$; that endpoint belongs to $E$ and is within $\ell _ n$. Hence $E$ has no isolated points. $\square$

Example 16.11.3 (Ordinary continuity and approximate continuity differ).C6F344E46714 The function $\chi _ E$ is discontinuous at every point of $E$ and continuous at every point outside $E$. Nevertheless its approximate value is one at almost every point of $E$ and zero at almost every point outside $E$. Thus a set of positive measure can consist entirely of ordinary discontinuities of a bounded measurable function that is approximately continuous almost everywhere.

Proof

Every neighborhood of a point of $E$ meets its dense open complement, where the function is zero, while its value on $E$ is one. Off the closed set $E$, a small neighborhood lies in its complement and the function is constant. The approximate-value statement is the Lebesgue density theorem applied to $E$ and its complement. More explicitly, at a density-one point of $E$, for every $0<\varepsilon <1$, the set $\{ |\chi _ E-1|>\varepsilon \} $ has density zero; this is the definition of approximate convergence to one. The analogous assertion gives zero off $E$ almost everywhere. No continuity representative is claimed. $\square$

Proposition 16.11.4 (The characteristic function has infinite variation).203445E7552B The function $\chi _ E$, extended by zero to $\mathbb R$, does not belong to $BV(\mathbb R)$; equivalently $E$ has infinite one-dimensional perimeter.

Proof

Fix a positive integer $N$. Choose a stage with at least $N+1$ retained components, and select $N+1$ successive such components. Each has positive $E$ measure by Proposition 16.11.2, so it contains a density-one point $x_ i$ of $E$, $0\le i\le N$. Between successive components choose a point $y_ i$ in a deleted open gap. In increasing order the selected points are $x_0<y_1<x_1<\cdots <y_ N<x_ N$.

Let $u_\varepsilon =\rho _\varepsilon *\chi _ E$ for a nonnegative smooth compactly supported approximate identity. At the chosen density points $u_\varepsilon (x_ i)\to 1$, and at the gap points $u_\varepsilon (y_ i)\to 0$. The fundamental theorem of calculus on each consecutive interval implies

\[ \int _{\mathbb R}|u_\varepsilon '|\ge \sum _{i=1}^ N\bigl (|u_\varepsilon (y_ i)-u_\varepsilon (x_{i-1})| +|u_\varepsilon (x_ i)-u_\varepsilon (y_ i)|\bigr )\longrightarrow 2N. \]

If $\chi _ E$ were BV, Lemma 4.2.3 would bound the left side by $|D\chi _ E|(\mathbb R)$ for all $\varepsilon $. Since $N$ is arbitrary this is impossible. The equivalence with perimeter is the definition of the perimeter of a measurable set via its characteristic function. $\square$

Example 16.11.5 (A finite-mass rectifiable current with infinite boundary mass).EDA509B2D116 Define a one-dimensional current on $\mathbb R$ by $T(f\, dx)=\int _ E f(x)\, dx$. It is an integer-multiplicity rectifiable current of mass $1/2$, but its boundary has infinite mass. Consequently it is neither normal nor integral.

Proof

The set $E$ is a measurable subset of the oriented line, with multiplicity one and length $1/2$, giving the rectifiable representation and mass. For a smooth compactly supported function $\varphi $, $\partial T(\varphi )=T(d\varphi )=\int _ E\varphi '$. If this zero-dimensional boundary current had finite mass, then

\[ \left|\int _ E\varphi '\right|\le C\| \varphi \| _\infty \]

for one finite constant $C$ and every such test. This is precisely bounded distributional variation of $\chi _ E$, contradicting Proposition 16.11.4. A normal current must have both finite mass and finite boundary mass; an integral current has locally integral boundary and, here with compact support, finite boundary mass. Thus neither property holds. This resolves the distinction posed in [Mor16], Exercise 4.17. $\square$
