16.29 Examples of coarea, multiplicity and projection8BCCB5A75445

8BCCB5A75445

These calculations illustrate the area and coarea conventions in [Sim18], Chapter 2, Sections 3 and 7, and Chapter 3, Sections 2–3. Each example specifies whether it counts points, measures a positive-dimensional fiber, or pushes an unweighted surface measure forward. Exceptional levels can carry large fibers even when every almost-everywhere conclusion is valid.

Example 16.29.1 (A tilted plane checks the coarea metric factor).49CF1B83DAE5 Let $M_ a=\{ (s,t,as):(s,t)\in (0,1)^2\} \subset \mathbb R^3$ and $f(x,y,z)=x$, with $a\in \mathbb R$. Then

\[ d\mathcal H^2\llcorner M_ a=\sqrt{1+a^2}\, ds\, dt, \qquad J_1(d^{M_ a}f)=\frac1{\sqrt{1+a^2}}. \]

For $0<u<1$, the fiber $M_ a\cap f^{-1}(u)$ has length one. Thus coarea gives $\int _{M_ a}J_1(d^{M_ a}f)\, d\mathcal H^2=1$, whereas the unweighted area is $\sqrt{1+a^2}$.

Proof

The graph derivative has tangent columns $(1,0,a)$ and $(0,1,0)$, which are orthogonal with squared lengths $1+a^2$ and one. Its area Jacobian is therefore $\sqrt{1+a^2}$. In the corresponding orthonormal tangent basis, the derivative of $f$ is the row $(1/\sqrt{1+a^2},0)$, giving the stated coarea Jacobian. A fiber has fixed $s=u$ and varies only in $t$, with unit speed and length one. Integrating either side of coarea gives one. Replacing the tangential Jacobian by the ambient gradient norm $|Df|=1$ would incorrectly give the larger unweighted area when $a\ne 0$. $\square$

Example 16.29.2 (Height on a round two-sphere has a constant area density).E66117D1B5C1 Let $R>0$ and let $z$ denote height on the sphere $S_ R^2\subset \mathbb R^3$. Away from its poles,

\[ |\nabla ^{S_ R^2}z|=\frac{\sqrt{R^2-z^2}}R, \qquad \mathcal H^1(S_ R^2\cap \{ z=t\} )=2\pi \sqrt{R^2-t^2} \quad (-R<t<R). \]

The pushforward of area by height is $2\pi R\, \chi _{(-R,R)}\mathcal L^1$. In particular the sphere has area $4\pi R^2$, and

\[ \int _{S_ R^2}|\nabla ^{S_ R^2}z|\, dA=\pi ^2R^2. \]
Proof

The unit normal is $x/R$. Projection of $e_3$ onto the tangent plane has norm $\sqrt{1-(z/R)^2}$, proving the gradient formula. The fiber is a horizontal circle of the indicated radius. The inverse-Jacobian formula in Proposition 3.12.2 gives the area pushforward density as the fiber length divided by this gradient norm, namely $2\pi R$. The two poles have zero area and contribute no critical pushforward mass. Integrating the constant density over $(-R,R)$ gives the area. Ordinary coarea gives the last integral as

\[ 2\pi \int _{-R}^ R\sqrt{R^2-t^2}\, dt=\pi ^2R^2, \]

since the integral is the area of a semicircle. Equivalently the parametrization $(\sqrt{R^2-t^2}\cos \theta ,\sqrt{R^2-t^2}\sin \theta ,t)$ has orthogonal derivatives with lengths $\sqrt{R^2-t^2}$ and $R/\sqrt{R^2-t^2}$, and hence constant area Jacobian $R$. The divergent reciprocal near the poles cancels the shrinking fiber length. $\square$

Example 16.29.3 (Squared radius on a disk gives a uniform pushforward).0D4EEA37173E Let $R>0$, $D_ R=\{ x\in \mathbb R^2:|x|<R\} $ and $f(x)=|x|^2$. Then

\[ f_\# (\mathcal L^2\llcorner D_ R)=\pi \chi _{(0,R^2)}\mathcal L^1. \]

For $0<t<R^2$, the fiber length is $2\pi \sqrt t$ and $|Df|=2\sqrt t$ on the fiber. The origin is critical but has zero area.

Proof

The derivative is $2x$, so the only critical point is the origin. Each positive fiber in the range is a circle of radius $\sqrt t$. Proposition 3.12.2 gives density $(2\pi \sqrt t)/(2\sqrt t)=\pi $ for the unweighted pushforward. There is no atom from the critical point because it has zero two-dimensional measure. Independently, the sublevel set $\{ f<t\} $ has area $\pi t$ for $0<t<R^2$, whose derivative is the same constant. Coarea with its Jacobian weight instead gives density $2\pi \sqrt t$; the two pushforwards must not be conflated. $\square$

Example 16.29.4 (A flat plateau produces a critical atom).EB1FFD21A1B7 On $D=(-1,1)\times (0,1)\subset \mathbb R^2$, let $f(x,y)=\max \{ x,0\} $. Its unweighted area pushforward is

\[ f_\# (\mathcal L^2\llcorner D)=\delta _0+\chi _{(0,1)}\mathcal L^1. \]

Its Jacobian-weighted pushforward is only $\chi _{(0,1)}\mathcal L^1$. The exceptional fiber $f^{-1}(0)$ has infinite $\mathcal H^1$ measure, although almost every fiber in the range has length one.

Proof

On $x<0$ the map is constant and its gradient is zero. This half-rectangle has area one and pushes to a unit atom at zero. On $x>0$ the gradient is $(1,0)$, and Fubini gives Lebesgue measure on $(0,1)$ as the pushforward. The line $x=0$ is a null set for area, so its nondifferentiability has no effect. Multiplication by the Jacobian removes the flat half-rectangle and yields the second pushforward.

The fiber at zero contains a nonempty open rectangle. Its one-dimensional Hausdorff measure is infinite: if a bounded set had finite $\mathcal H^1$, diameter covers would give zero two-dimensional Lebesgue measure by multiplying their one-dimensional cost by the maximum diameter and letting that diameter tend to zero. This contradicts the positive area of the half-rectangle. For $t\in (0,1)$ the fiber is a vertical unit segment, and other nonzero levels have empty fibers. An infinite fiber value at one Lebesgue-null level does not affect the coarea integral. This example shows why the critical contribution in Proposition 3.12.2 must be retained. $\square$

Example 16.29.5 (Distance is constant on a sphere and its volume function jumps).812BD5E64F51 Let $R>0$, $M=S_ R^2\subset \mathbb R^3$ and $\rho (x)=|x|$. Its tangential gradient vanishes everywhere, while

\[ \rho _\# (\mathcal H^2\llcorner M)=4\pi R^2\delta _ R. \]

The function $V(t)=\mathcal H^2(M\cap B_ t(0))$ equals zero for $t\leq R$ and $4\pi R^2$ for $t>R$. It is not absolutely continuous. Coarea nevertheless holds: all level intersections except the single level $R$ are empty.

Proof

The function $\rho |M$ is the constant $R$, so its tangential derivative is zero and its unweighted pushforward is the claimed atom. The area is $4\pi R^2$ by Example 16.29.2. The strict inequality in the open-ball definition accounts for $V(R)=0$. At the one nonempty level the fiber is the entire two-sphere and has infinite one-dimensional measure, by the same dimension comparison as in Example 16.29.4, applied to a regular chart. That value occurs on a null set of radii, and the integral of the fiber length is zero, agreeing with the zero Jacobian integral. Proposition 3.12.7 concerns the empty regular part in this example and makes no incorrect absolute-continuity claim for $V$ itself. $\square$

Example 16.29.6 (A fold counts two preimages rather than one image).36E81AC368FB For $f:(-1,1)\to \mathbb R$, $f(s)=s^2$, the image has length one, but

\[ \int _{-1}^1|f'(s)|\, ds=2 =\int _0^1\# f^{-1}(t)\, dt. \]

The unweighted pushforward of $ds$ has density $t^{-1/2}\chi _{(0,1)}(t)$, which is integrable despite its singularity at zero. This is an equal-dimensional area-formula example, not a positive-dimensional coarea fiber.

Proof

For $0<t<1$ there are two preimages $\pm \sqrt t$, and outside $[0,1)$ there are none. The unique preimage at zero does not affect the integral. Direct integration gives $\int _{-1}^1 2|s|\, ds=2$, matching the multiplicity integral. For the unweighted pushforward, apply the weighted area formula with weight $1/|f'|$ away from the null point zero. At each positive target value the two inverse derivative factors are each $1/(2\sqrt t)$, summing to $1/\sqrt t$. Its integral over $(0,1)$ is two, the original interval length. Thus counting the image once would lose one sheet, while omitting the inverse derivative would describe a different pushforward measure. $\square$

Example 16.29.7 (A segment and a separated dust realize both decomposition parts).508F24AC4E8F Let $L=[0,1]\times \{ 0\} $ and let $K'=K+(0,2)$ be a translated four-corner set. For $A=L\cup K'$, its rectifiable part is $L$ and its purely one-unrectifiable part is $K'$, modulo $\mathcal H^1$-null sets. Both parts have positive finite length.

Proof

The segment is a compact Lipschitz image of an interval and has length one. The translated four-corner set has positive finite length and is purely one-unrectifiable by Theorem 16.12.7. Translation preserves those properties. The two sets are disjoint by their height ranges, so they already give a decomposition of the required type. Uniqueness in Theorem 3.11.5 identifies them with the canonical measure classes of the two parts. In particular no positive-length portion of the dust can be absorbed into a different rectifiable part by changing the chosen representative. $\square$

Example 16.29.8 (One null coordinate projection does not imply pure unrectifiability).0B1ACA8FF719 For $1\leq m<n$, consider

\[ M=\operatorname {span}\{ e_1,\ldots ,e_{m-1},e_{m+1}\} \subset \mathbb R^ n, \]

with the empty initial list allowed when $m=1$. Projection onto $\operatorname {span}\{ e_1,\ldots ,e_ m\} $ has $m$-dimensional image measure zero, although $M$ is a smooth rectifiable $m$-plane. Projection onto its own coordinate plane is the identity.

Proof

The first projection maps $M$ to the $(m-1)$-plane spanned by $e_1,\ldots ,e_{m-1}$. This lower-dimensional plane has zero $\mathcal H^ m$ measure, as follows from diameter covers of bounded pieces. Yet the plane $M$ itself has positive $m$-measure on every nonempty relative ball, and is countably rectifiable by its linear parametrization. It is therefore not purely $m$-unrectifiable. The second coordinate projection has Jacobian one on $M$. This explains why Theorem 3.11.8 requires all coordinate $m$-projections, including when $n<2m$ so a fully orthogonal $m$-plane would not exist. $\square$
