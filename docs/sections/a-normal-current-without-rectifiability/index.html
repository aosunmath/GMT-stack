16.14 A normal current without rectifiabilityAA5865D2C194

AA5865D2C194

Normality controls a current and its boundary by measures, but does not force its mass to lie on a set of its own dimension. The following elementary example also makes visible the distinction between Euclidean and mass norms in [Sim18], Chapter 6, Remark 2.7, printed p. 160.

Example 16.14.1 (A nonsimple bivector and its two norms).5584C114BE31 In oriented $\mathbb R^4$, put $\zeta =e_1\wedge e_2+e_3\wedge e_4$. Its Euclidean norm is $\sqrt2$, while its mass norm is $2$. It is not simple.

Proof

The two basis bivectors are orthonormal, giving the Euclidean norm. Their individual mass norms are one, so the triangle inequality gives $|\zeta |_{\mathrm{mass}}\leq 2$. For the reverse inequality, let $\omega _0=dx^1\wedge dx^2+dx^3\wedge dx^4$. Define the orthogonal complex structure $Je_1=e_2$, $Je_2=-e_1$, $Je_3=e_4$, $Je_4=-e_3$. Then $\omega _0(u,v)=\langle Ju,v\rangle $, whose absolute value is at most one on orthonormal pairs. Equality holds at $(e_1,e_2)$, so $\omega _0$ has comass one. As $\langle \omega _0,\zeta \rangle =2$, duality gives the opposite mass bound. Finally $\zeta \wedge \zeta =2e_1\wedge e_2\wedge e_3\wedge e_4\ne 0$, whereas the square of a simple bivector is zero. $\square$

Example 16.14.2 (A diffuse finite-mass two-current).4B74DFD48D85 Let $Q=[0,1]^4$ and define a two-current on $\mathbb R^4$ by

\[ T(\omega )=\int _ Q\bigl (\omega _{12}(x)+\omega _{34}(x)\bigr )\, dx. \]

Then $\| T\| =2\mathcal L^4\llcorner Q$, its polar bivector is $\zeta /2$, and $\mathbf M(T)=2$. Its support is $Q$, and its mass measure is non-atomic.

Proof

The bound $|\langle \omega ,\zeta \rangle |\leq 2\| \omega \| _*$ shows that the formula is a current and bounds its mass by $2\mathcal L^4\llcorner Q$. For any open $W$ and any smooth scalar cutoff $0\leq g\leq 1$ compactly supported in $W$, the form $g\omega _0$ has comass at most one and $T(g\omega _0)=2\int _ Qg\, dx$. Taking the supremum of these cutoffs yields the reverse open-set mass bound. This identifies the measure and the polar field. Every ball about a point of $Q$ meets $Q$ in positive four-dimensional measure, while tests outside $Q$ vanish; hence the support is $Q$. Singletons have zero Lebesgue measure. $\square$

Proposition 16.14.3 (The diffuse current has a finite-mass boundary).852428C98493 The current in Example 16.14.2 is normal and satisfies $\mathbf M(\partial T)\leq 8$.

Proof

For a smooth compactly supported one-form $\eta =\sum _ i\eta _ i\, dx^ i$,

\[ \partial T(\eta )=\int _ Q(\partial _1\eta _2-\partial _2\eta _1 +\partial _3\eta _4-\partial _4\eta _3)\, dx. \]

Apply the fundamental theorem of calculus in the differentiated coordinate and Fubini's theorem to each of the four terms. The result is a sum of eight integrals over unit three-dimensional faces, each of a single component of $\eta $, with its appropriate sign. If the comass of $\eta $ is at most one, each component is bounded by one, so the absolute value is at most eight. Taking the supremum proves the boundary mass bound. Together with $\mathbf M(T)=2$, this proves normality. The face integrals define a one-current distributed over three-dimensional faces; they are not integration currents of those faces in dimension three. $\square$

Lemma 16.14.4 (Lower-dimensional Lipschitz images have zero ambient volume).FF7C6E81ADF7 If $m<N$ and $f:A\subset \mathbb R^ m\to \mathbb R^ N$ is Lipschitz, then $\mathcal L^ N(f(A))=0$, where outer measure suffices if measurability has not been specified.

Proof

First suppose $A\subset [-R,R]^ m$ and let $L$ be a Lipschitz constant. Cover this cube by at most $C_ R\delta ^{-m}$ cubes of side $\delta $. For each cube meeting $A$, choose a point of $A$ in it. Its image and the image of the portion of $A$ in that cube lie in a ball of radius $L\sqrt m\delta $ (if $L=0$, the image is a singleton). The sum of the volumes of these covering balls is at most $C\delta ^{N-m}$, tending to zero. Thus the outer measure vanishes. Intersect an unbounded domain with countably many bounded cubes to obtain the general case. $\square$

Proposition 16.14.5 (Normality does not imply rectifiability).D93B08103DE9 The current in Example 16.14.2 is not a rectifiable two-current, even with real multiplicities.

Proof

The mass of a rectifiable two-current is concentrated on a countably two-rectifiable set $M$. Such a set is covered, up to an $\mathcal H^2$-null set, by countably many Lipschitz images from subsets of $\mathbb R^2$. Lemma 16.14.4 makes all these images $\mathcal L^4$-null. The remaining $\mathcal H^2$-null set is also $\mathcal L^4$-null: covers with arbitrarily small diameters satisfy $\sum _ i(\operatorname {diam}U_ i)^4\leq \delta ^2\sum _ i(\operatorname {diam}U_ i)^2$. Hence $\mathcal L^4(M)=0$. But the explicitly computed mass measure $2\mathcal L^4\llcorner Q$ assigns zero mass to $M$ and total mass two to $Q$. It cannot be concentrated on $M$. Thus the current is not rectifiable, despite its finite mass and finite boundary mass. $\square$

Proposition 16.14.6 (Rectifiable normal currents can converge to a diffuse current).7038E9854215 For each positive integer $j$, let $P_ j$ be the $j^2$ centers of the equal grid squares in $[0,1]^2$. Let $S_ p^{12}$ be the integration current of $[0,1]^2\times \{ p\} $ with orientation $e_1\wedge e_2$, and let $S_ p^{34}$ be that of $\{ p\} \times [0,1]^2$ with orientation $e_3\wedge e_4$. Then

\[ T_ j=j^{-2}\sum _{p\in P_ j}(S_ p^{12}+S_ p^{34}) \]

are compactly supported rectifiable normal two-currents with real multiplicities, $\mathbf M(T_ j)=2$ and $\mathbf M(\partial T_ j)=8$. They converge weakly to the nonrectifiable current $T$ of Example 16.14.2. Their multiplicities are not integer-valued for $j>1$.

Proof

Each current is a finite real linear combination of oriented squares. Within each family the squares are disjoint. A square in one family intersects a square in the other in at most one point, a set of zero two-dimensional measure. Their positive mass measures therefore add and give $2j^2j^{-2}=2$. Each square has boundary mass four. The grid centers are in the interior of the transverse square, so boundary edges from the two families do not meet; within one family they are also disjoint. Thus the boundary masses add to eight.

For a smooth test form $\omega $, the first-family contribution is the midpoint Riemann sum for the continuous function

\[ p\longmapsto \int _{[0,1]^2}\omega _{12}(x_1,x_2,p_1,p_2)\, dx_1dx_2. \]

It converges to $\int _ Q\omega _{12}\, dx$. The second family gives $\int _ Q\omega _{34}\, dx$. Their sum is $T(\omega )$, proving weak convergence. Almost every point on any one square lies on no other square, and there the multiplicity is $j^{-2}$. For $j>1$ it is not an integer. This example is consistent with integral-current compactness: omitting integer multiplicities changes the conclusion, even with fixed compact support and uniform mass and boundary-mass bounds. $\square$

Proposition 16.14.7 (The diffuse approximation also converges in the real flat norm).2AAA27455AE0 For the currents $T_ j$ of Proposition 16.14.6, the real-current flat norm of Definition 5.3.14 satisfies

\[ \mathcal F_{\mathbb R}(T_ j-T)\leq \frac{5\sqrt2}{j}. \]

Thus even flat convergence, fixed compact support, and uniform mass and boundary-mass bounds do not preserve real rectifiability without an additional hypothesis.

Proof

For $y\in [0,1]^2$, write $S_ y^{12}$ for the corresponding oriented square, and let $p_ j(y)$ be the center of its grid cell. Values on grid boundaries may be assigned arbitrarily. The displacement is at most $\delta _ j=\sqrt2/(2j)$. Translating $S_ y^{12}$ along the segment from $y$ to $p_ j(y)$ gives the homotopy decomposition of Proposition 5.3.13:

\[ S_{p_ j(y)}^{12}-S_ y^{12}=\partial R_ y+B_ y, \qquad \mathbf M(R_ y)\leq \delta _ j, \qquad \mathbf M(B_ y)\leq 4\delta _ j. \]

Here $R_ y$ is the image of the interval product with the square, and $B_ y$ is the image of the interval product with its boundary. The estimates are exactly Proposition 5.3.17 with Lipschitz constant one, square mass one and boundary mass four.

Define averaged currents by integrating their test-form evaluations over $y$. These evaluations are measurable (smooth on each cell apart from its boundary), and all supports lie in the fixed cube $Q$. The uniform mass bounds imply that the integrals define finite-mass currents $R$ and $B$, with masses at most $\delta _ j$ and $4\delta _ j$. Their boundary identity follows by evaluating $R$ on the exterior derivative of a test form and interchanging the finite integrals. The average of $S_{p_ j(y)}^{12}$ is the first-family Riemann sum, while the average of $S_ y^{12}$ is the first summand of $T$. Thus this family's difference is $\partial R+B$ and has real flat norm at most $5\delta _ j$. Repeat for the second family and add the decompositions. The total bound is $10\delta _ j=5\sqrt2/j$. No integer-coefficient filling or integer flat-norm assertion is involved. $\square$
