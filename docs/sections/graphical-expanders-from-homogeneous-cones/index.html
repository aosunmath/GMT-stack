16.25 Graphical expanders from homogeneous cones828E77EB129A

828E77EB129A

The one-dimensional corner construction has a counterpart in every dimension. Global existence for Lipschitz graphs is the external input from [EH89], Theorem 4.6. Scaling and the bounded-slope comparison theorem then give the expander and its uniqueness. This argument concerns entire graphical hypersurfaces; it makes no assertion about the number of nongraphical expanders asymptotic to the same geometric cone.

Theorem 16.25.1 (A unique bounded-slope graphical expander for a Lipschitz cone).9EC09394BFF2 Let $f:\mathbb R^ n\to \mathbb R$, $n\geq 1$, be globally $L$-Lipschitz and positively homogeneous of degree one: $f(\lambda x)=\lambda f(x)$ for every $\lambda >0$. There is a unique smooth entire graph $U$ with bounded slope whose dilation flow $\sqrt t\, U(x/\sqrt t)$ converges locally uniformly to $f$ as $t\downarrow 0$ and solves graphical mean curvature flow. It satisfies

\[ \left(\delta _{ij}-\frac{U_ iU_ j}{1+|DU|^2}\right)U_{ij} =\frac{U-x\cdot DU}{2},\qquad |DU|\leq L, \qquad |U(x)-f(x)|\leq L\sqrt{2n}. \]

Its graph is properly embedded and complete. The existence input is [EH89], Theorem 4.6; the self-similarity and the stated uniqueness follow from the local comparison argument in this project.

Proof

Use Theorem 14.21.7 to obtain a smooth positive-time entire flow $u$ with initial trace $f$ and $|Du|\leq L$. For every $\lambda >0$ the parabolic rescaling

\[ u_\lambda (x,t)=\lambda ^{-1}u(\lambda x,\lambda ^2t) \]

solves the same graph equation, has the same slope bound, and has initial trace $\lambda ^{-1}f(\lambda x)=f(x)$. On each finite common time interval, Theorem 14.24.5 gives $u_\lambda =u$. Taking $U=u(\cdot ,1)$ therefore yields $u(x,t)=\sqrt t\, U(x/\sqrt t)$. Differentiate this identity and substitute into the graph equation to obtain the displayed elliptic equation with the positive expander sign. The slope estimate is inherited from the flow, and Theorem 14.25.3 at time one gives the height estimate.

An entire smooth graph is properly embedded: a compact ambient set bounds the base variable, and the graph is closed. Its induced metric dominates the Euclidean base metric. An intrinsic Cauchy sequence thus converges in the base; smoothness on a compact base neighborhood gives convergence in the graph metric. Hence the graph is complete. Finally, another profile in the stated class has a positive-time dilation flow with bounded slope and the same initial trace. Theorem 14.24.5 identifies the flows, hence their time-one profiles. Existence is not being reconstructed from the elliptic equation alone. $\square$

Proposition 16.25.2 (Order, symmetries and blow-down of cone expanders).35C79DC18B68 Write $U_ f$ for the profile in Theorem 16.25.1. If $f\leq g$, then $U_ f\leq U_ g$. For $Q\in O(n)$ one has $U_{f\circ Q}=U_ f\circ Q$, and $U_{-f}=-U_ f$. In particular all orthogonal symmetries of the cone are inherited. The quantitative blow-down estimate is

\[ \sup _{x\in \mathbb R^ n}|R^{-1}U_ f(Rx)-f(x)| \leq L\sqrt{2n}/R\qquad (R>0). \]

If $f(x)=p\cdot x$, then $U_ f(x)=p\cdot x$.

Proof

Order follows by applying Theorem 14.24.5 to the flows from the two cones. Orthogonal changes of the base and reflection in the height coordinate preserve the graph equation, and give the indicated initial traces. Uniqueness identifies the transformed flows with the corresponding constructed ones. Divide the uniform height estimate in Theorem 16.25.1 at $Rx$ by $R$ and use homogeneity to prove the blow-down estimate. Finally the linear function is a stationary graphical flow and solves the expander equation because both its Hessian and $U-x\cdot DU$ vanish. Uniqueness gives the last assertion. A vertical translate of a linear profile would not satisfy this origin-centered expander equation unless the added constant were zero. $\square$

Example 16.25.3 (Rotational expanders from circular graphical cones).E19F15FD3062 For $\ell >0$, the cone $f(x)=\ell |x|$ produces a radial profile $U(x)=w(r)$, $r=|x|$. For $r>0$,

\[ \frac{w''}{1+(w')^2}+\frac{n-1}{r}w' =\frac{w-rw'}2. \]

At its smooth center, $w'(0)=0$ and, writing $c=w(0)$,

\[ 0<c\leq \ell \sqrt{2n},\qquad w''(0)=\frac{c}{2n}, \qquad |A|(0)=\frac{c}{2\sqrt n}. \]

The dilation flow has tip height $c\sqrt t$ and curvature $c/(2\sqrt{nt})$ at its tip. Thus the one-half time scale occurs in radial hypersurface examples in every dimension.

Proof

Orthogonal symmetry in Proposition 16.25.2 makes $U$ radial. At $r>0$ its Hessian has eigenvalues $w''$ in the radial direction and $w'/r$ in the $n-1$ orthogonal directions, while the coefficient matrix has radial eigenvalue $(1+(w')^2)^{-1}$ and tangential eigenvalues one. This gives the equation. Smooth radial symmetry gives $DU(0)=0$ and $D^2U(0)=\alpha I$. The equation at the origin gives $n\alpha =c/2$.

Comparison with the zero cone gives $u\geq 0$. Its initial trace is positive away from the origin. At any fixed positive time the solution is strictly positive by the classical local strong maximum principle: on positive-time cylinders the graph equation is a smooth uniformly parabolic scalar equation, and an interior zero would force zero at earlier positive times in the connected domain. Chaining overlapping bounded cylinders contradicts locally uniform convergence to the nonzero initial trace. This standard parabolic principle is used as background, not proved here. Hence $c>0$. The height bound at the origin gives $c\leq \ell \sqrt{2n}$. Since the graph metric at the origin is Euclidean, the second fundamental form has $n$ equal principal curvatures in absolute value $c/(2n)$; its norm is $c/(2\sqrt n)$. Dilation by $\sqrt t$ proves both time formulas. No global convexity or classification of all rotational immersions is needed for these conclusions. $\square$

Example 16.25.4 (A nonconvex cone with an expander of changing height sign).AB32BF435231 In dimension two take $f(x_1,x_2)=|x_1|-|x_2|$. Its unique bounded-slope expander is even in each coordinate and satisfies

\[ U(x_2,x_1)=-U(x_1,x_2). \]

It vanishes on both diagonals, is not affine, and takes both positive and negative values. At the origin $U=0$, $DU=0$, $H=0$ and $D^2U=\operatorname {diag}(a,-a)$ for some real $a$. No value or nonvanishing assertion for $a$ is required.

Proof

The cone is positively one-homogeneous and $\sqrt2$-Lipschitz. Its separate even symmetries and sign reversal under swapping coordinates transfer to $U$ by Proposition 16.25.2. They force the stated vanishing on the diagonals and the values of $U$, its gradient, and the shape of its Hessian at the origin. The expander equation gives zero mean curvature there. The uniform height estimate gives $|U-f|\leq 2\sqrt2$, so $U(R,0)>0$ and $U(0,R)<0$ for large $R$. If $U$ were affine, its blow-down would be a linear function, contradicting the explicit nonaffine cone. Moreover its scalar mean curvature with the upward orientation satisfies $H(x_2,x_1)=-H(x_1,x_2)$, by the formula $H=-\operatorname {div}(DU/v)$. If $H$ had one sign it would vanish identically. The expander equation would then give $U=x\cdot DU$, making $U$ homogeneous of degree one; smoothness at the origin forces such a function to be linear, a contradiction. Thus the mean curvature changes sign. This example does not classify its zero set beyond the two diagonals, or infer a sign for each principal curvature away from the origin. $\square$

Corollary 16.25.5 (Convex cones have strictly positive expander defect).2F1C6D2E9397 If the cone $f$ in Theorem 16.25.1 is convex and not linear, then its profile satisfies

\[ U_ f(x)>f(x),\qquad U_ f(x)-x\cdot DU_ f(x)>0,\qquad H<0 \]

for the upward orientation. In particular $U_ f(0)>0$. These conclusions hold in every dimension and distinguish convex cones from Example 16.25.4.

Proof

Proposition 14.24.7 gives strict height increase and $u_ t>0$ for the constructed flow. Its self-similar expression gives $2u_ t(x,1)=U_ f(x)-x\cdot DU_ f(x)$. This proves the defect inequality and the sign of $H=-u_ t/v$. A positively one-homogeneous continuous function has $f(0)=0$, so the strict height inequality yields $U_ f(0)>0$. The proof does not require a separate convexity-preservation theorem for the flow. $\square$
