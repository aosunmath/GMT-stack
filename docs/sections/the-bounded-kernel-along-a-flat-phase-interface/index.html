12.9 The bounded kernel along a flat phase interface1A650FD2D1D2

1A650FD2D1D2

Write $x=(y,t)\in \mathbb R^ d\times \mathbb R$, $d\geq 1$, and retain $\psi =q'$, $V=W''(q)$ from Section 12.8. We develop the bounded-kernel argument of [ChAC], Appendix B, Problem 2(d)–(g), pp. 28–29. Interior elliptic derivative estimates are explicitly retained as an analytic input in the decay step.

Lemma 12.9.1 (A bounded positive subsolution cannot persist on all of Euclidean space).0A781005EAC2 If $G\in C^2(\mathbb R^ d)$ is bounded above and $\Delta G\geq cG$ for a constant $c>0$, then $G\leq 0$ everywhere. In particular a nonnegative bounded such function is zero.

Proof

If $G(y_0)>0$, choose $\eta >0$ with $2d\eta <cG(y_0)$. The function $G(y)-\eta |y-y_0|^2$ tends to minus infinity at infinity and attains its maximum at some $y_\eta $. At that point $G(y_\eta )\geq G(y_0)$ and $\Delta G(y_\eta )\leq 2d\eta $. These inequalities contradict $\Delta G\geq cG$. This proof uses only a penalized maximum on the full Euclidean domain; the conclusion need not hold on a bounded domain with positive boundary data. $\square$

Lemma 12.9.2 (Uniform decay in the normal direction).53B06602A64D Let $w\in C^2(\mathbb R^{d+1})\cap L^\infty $ solve

\[ \Delta _ yw+w_{tt}-V(t)w=0. \]

For every $0<\sigma <\sqrt2$ there is $C_\sigma $ such that

\[ |w(y,t)|+|Dw(y,t)|+|D^2w(y,t)| \leq C_\sigma e^{-\sigma |t|} \]

for every $(y,t)$. The constants are uniform in the tangential variable $y$.

Proof

Choose $0<\delta <1$ and then $\Lambda $ so large that $V(t)>\max \{ \sigma ^2,2\delta ^2\} $ for $|t|\geq \Lambda $. On the upper half-space $t\geq \Lambda $, put

\[ B_\eta (y,t)=e^{\sigma (\Lambda -t)}+ \eta \cosh (\delta t)\sum _{i=1}^ d\cosh (\delta y_ i),\qquad \eta >0. \]

The operator $P=\Delta _ y+\partial _ t^2-V(t)$ is strictly negative on each positive summand. In particular the product summands have Laplacian $2\delta ^2$ times themselves; the coefficient is not multiplied by the number of tangential dimensions. The barrier is at least one on $t=\Lambda $ and tends to infinity along every sequence escaping to infinity in the half-space.

Let $M=\| w\| _\infty $. If $M=0$ there is nothing to prove. Each of $\pm w-MB_\eta $ is nonpositive on the finite boundary, negative outside a sufficiently large compact set, and has $P(\pm w-MB_\eta )>0$. A positive interior maximum would have $P(\pm w-MB_\eta )<0$ because $V>0$, a contradiction. Therefore $|w|\leq MB_\eta $. Send $\eta \downarrow 0$ at a fixed point to obtain $|w(y,t)|\leq Me^{\sigma (\Lambda -t)}$. The lower half-space is identical, and the remaining strip is handled by increasing the constant.

The coefficient $V$ and all its derivatives are uniformly bounded. Interior elliptic estimates on unit balls, followed by bootstrapping, bound first and second derivatives by a constant times the supremum of $|w|$ on a slightly larger ball. These estimates are the external local elliptic input used here. The proved exponential bound on that larger ball differs by at most a fixed factor, so it gives the displayed derivative bounds uniformly in $y$. This justifies the decay step requested in [ChAC], Problem 2(e). $\square$

Proposition 12.9.3 (The translation coefficient is constant).8FC283F8B3D2 For a bounded solution as in Lemma 12.9.2, define

\[ a(y)=\frac1{c_ W}\int _{\mathbb R}w(y,t)\psi (t)dt. \]

Then $a$ is bounded and harmonic on $\mathbb R^ d$, and hence constant. The remainder $v=w-a\psi $ solves the same linearized equation and satisfies $\int v(y,t)\psi (t)dt=0$ for every $y$.

Proof

The decay estimates and the exponential decay of $\psi $ justify two tangential derivatives under the integral and integrations by parts in $t$, with vanishing endpoint terms. Thus

\[ c_ W\Delta _ y a=\int (Vw-w_{tt})\psi =\int w(V\psi -\psi '')=0. \]

Boundedness follows either from the decay or directly from bounded $w$ and integrable $\psi $. To recall the needed Liouville argument, the mean-value formula for a bounded harmonic function and differentiation of the moving ball give $|D a(y)|\leq C_ d\| a\| _\infty /R$ for every $R>0$. This can also be seen by writing the derivative of its ball average as a boundary integral. Let $R\to \infty $ to conclude $Da=0$. Subtracting the constant multiple of the kernel function gives both remaining assertions. The projection is defined before any conclusion about the entire kernel; no separation-of-variables assumption is used. $\square$

Theorem 12.9.4 (Bounded solutions of the flat linearized equation).4372C7E46BA5 Every bounded $C^2$ solution of

\[ \Delta w-W''(q(x_{d+1}))w=0 \quad \text{on }\mathbb R^{d+1} \]

is $w(y,t)=a\psi (t)$ for one constant $a$. Together with Proposition 12.8.2, this holds in every ambient dimension at least one. This is the kernel assertion in [ChAC], Appendix B, Problem 2, with its normal decay and orthogonality steps made explicit.

Proof

Take the orthogonal remainder $v$ from Proposition 12.9.3 and define $G(y)=\int _{\mathbb R}v(y,t)^2dt$. It is nonnegative, bounded and twice continuously differentiable by Lemma 12.9.2. Differentiation, the equation and integration by parts give the exact identity

\[ \Delta _ yG=2\int |D_ yv|^2dt+ 2\int \bigl (|v_ t|^2+V(t)v^2\bigr )dt. \]

Each normal slice lies in $H^1$ and is orthogonal to $\psi $. Theorem 12.8.4 therefore implies

\[ \Delta _ yG\geq 2\int |D_ yv|^2dt+3G\geq 3G. \]

Lemma 12.9.1 forces $G=0$. Continuity then gives $v=0$, proving the claim. The displayed gradient term is tangential; the inequality is derived from the exact slice energy rather than by discarding the potentially negative normal potential. $\square$

Proposition 12.9.5 (Splitting the full quadratic form into translation and normal parts).4A01EE1BF148 For $f\in H^1(\mathbb R^ d\times \mathbb R)$ set

\[ a(y)=c_ W^{-1}\int f(y,t)\psi (t)dt,\qquad v(y,t)=f(y,t)-a(y)\psi (t). \]

Then $a\in H^1(\mathbb R^ d)$, $v\in H^1$, and

\[ \int \bigl (|Df|^2+Vf^2\bigr ) =c_ W\int |D_ ya|^2+ \int _{\mathbb R^ d}\left(\int _{\mathbb R}|D_ yv|^2dt+Q(v(y,\cdot ))\right)dy. \]

In particular the rightmost term is at least $\int |D_ yv|^2+(3/2)\int v^2$.

Proof

Cauchy–Schwarz in the normal variable bounds $a$ and each weak tangential derivative in $L^2$. Since $\psi ,\psi '\in L^2$, the product $a\psi $ lies in $H^1$, hence so does $v$. The normal pairing with $\psi $ is zero, including after a tangential weak derivative. Therefore tangential cross terms vanish on integrating in $t$. The normal cross term is $Q(\psi ,v)=0$ by the factorization, and $Q(\psi )=0$. Expanding the quadratic form proves the identity, first for smooth approximations and then by $H^1$ continuity. Apply the sharp normal gap on almost every slice and integrate by Fubini to obtain the estimate. $\square$

Example 12.9.6 (A normal gap does not give a full-space spectral gap).09EF9F42AC39 For $d\geq 1$, the full quadratic form of Proposition 12.9.5 is strictly positive on every nonzero $H^1(\mathbb R^{d+1})$ function, but its Rayleigh infimum is zero. Consequently it has no nonzero $H^1$ null vector despite the bounded translation solution $\psi (t)$.

Proof

If the form is zero, the splitting forces $v=0$ and $D_ ya=0$. A constant in $L^2(\mathbb R^ d)$ is zero, proving strict positivity. For a nonzero $\eta \in C_ c^\infty (\mathbb R^ d)$, take $f_ R(y,t)=\eta (y/R)\psi (t)$. Its Rayleigh quotient is

\[ R^{-2}\frac{\int |D\eta |^2}{\int \eta ^2}\longrightarrow 0. \]

These are $H^1$ functions; cutting off the exponentially decaying normal factor approximates them by compactly supported tests with the same limiting quotients. The bounded function $\psi (t)$ is not in $L^2(\mathbb R^{d+1})$ because the tangential factor has infinite volume. This separates bounded-kernel rigidity, normal coercivity and a positive spectral gap on the whole space. $\square$
