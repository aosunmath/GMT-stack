10.6 The logarithmic cost of a Euclidean catenoidal neckD769746506C0

D769746506C0

This section develops the explicit Euclidean calculation in [KMN], Section 2.1, Proposition 2.1 and Remark 2.2, pp. 4–6 of the supplied manuscript. It explains the scale in Remark 10.1.3. It does not construct the manifold sweepout of that paper's Theorem 2.4, and it makes no conclusion about multiplicity in an unspecified min-max class.

Proposition 10.6.1 (Two rotational catenoids with the same circular boundary).81AF723B08AA Fix $R>0$ and $h>0$. A rotational catenoid of waist radius $a>0$, centered at height zero, meets the planes $z=\pm h$ in circles of radius $R$ exactly when

\[ R=a\cosh (h/a). \]

There is a unique $t_*>0$ satisfying $t_*\tanh t_*=1$. Set $q_*=t_*/\cosh t_*$. If $0<h/R<q_*$, there are exactly two such waist radii. At equality there is exactly one, and above it there is none. The smaller waist corresponds to the larger solution $t=h/a>t_*$. The same threshold parameter occurs in the free-boundary catenoid of Example 16.7.5; the present assertion counts rotational solutions for prescribed boundary circles.

Proof

The catenoid parametrization in Example 16.2.2 has radius $a\cosh v$ at height $av$, giving the equation. On putting $t=h/a$, it becomes $h/R=t/\cosh t$. The derivative of the right side is $(1-t\tanh t)/\cosh t$. The function $t\tanh t$ is strictly increasing from zero to infinity, since its derivative is $\tanh t+t/\cosh ^2t>0$. Thus the ratio increases strictly to a unique maximum and then decreases strictly to zero; its limit at zero is also zero. The number of roots follows by the intermediate value theorem and strict monotonicity. Since $a=h/t$, the larger root has the smaller waist. This argument counts rotational solutions and does not assert uniqueness among all surfaces spanning the two circles, or prove their stability indices. $\square$

Proposition 10.6.2 (Exact excess over two disks).6672A8E926D4 For a truncated catenoid with waist $a$ and parameter range $|v|\leq T$, put $h=aT$ and $R=a\cosh T$. Its area $A$ and excess over the two radius-$R$ disks are

\[ A=2\pi a^2(T+\sinh T\cosh T),\qquad A-2\pi R^2=2\pi a^2\left(T-\frac12-\frac12e^{-2T}\right). \]

In particular the excess is positive for all sufficiently large $T$, although its ratio to $h^2$ tends to zero as $T\to \infty $.

Proof

The induced metric is $a^2\cosh ^2v(dv^2+d\theta ^2)$. Integrating the area density over a full angular period and $[-T,T]$ gives the first formula. For the second, subtract $2\pi a^2\cosh ^2T$ and use

\[ \sinh T\cosh T-\cosh ^2T=-\cosh T e^{-T} =-\frac12(1+e^{-2T}). \]

Finally divide the excess by $h^2=a^2T^2$. The resulting expression is $2\pi (T-1/2-e^{-2T}/2)/T^2$, positive eventually and tending to zero. No limiting interchange with an area-minimizing problem is involved. $\square$

Theorem 10.6.3 (The sharp leading logarithmic neck cost).AB484D5A03C6 Fix $R>0$ and let $h\downarrow 0$. Choose the smaller waist solution $a(h)$ of Proposition 10.6.1. If $L=\log (R/h)$ and $T=h/a(h)$, then

\[ \frac{T}{L}\longrightarrow 1,\qquad T=L+\log L+\log 2+o(1),\qquad \frac{\bigl (A(h)-2\pi R^2\bigr )L}{h^2}\longrightarrow 2\pi . \]

Consequently, for every $\varepsilon >0$ and all sufficiently small $h>0$,

\[ A(h)\leq 2\pi R^2+\frac{2\pi (1+\varepsilon )h^2}{\log (R/h)}. \]

This is the dimensionless version of the leading estimate in [KMN], Proposition 2.1 and Remark 2.2; it concerns the explicit rotational neck.

Proof

The larger root $T$ tends to infinity: on the strictly decreasing branch of $t/\cosh t$, its value $h/R$ tends to zero, and no bounded sequence on that branch can have this limit. The boundary equation yields

\[ L=\log \cosh T-\log T =T-\log 2+\log (1+e^{-2T})-\log T. \]

Divide by $T$ to obtain $L/T\to 1$. Rearrangement and $\log T-\log L\to 0$ then give the second expansion. By Proposition 10.6.2,

\[ \frac{(A-2\pi R^2)L}{h^2} =2\pi \frac{L}{T}\left(1-\frac{1+e^{-2T}}{2T}\right)\longrightarrow 2\pi . \]

The asserted bound is the definition of this limit, with $L>0$ once $h<R$. The logarithm uses a ratio of lengths, so it is invariant under a common change of length units. $\square$

Example 10.6.4 (A cylindrical connector has a larger leading cost).2AE5C3C55721 For $0<h<R$, remove a disk of radius $\rho \in [0,R]$ from each of the planar radius-$R$ disks at heights $\pm h$, and join the inner boundary circles by a cylinder of radius $\rho $. The resulting piecewise smooth comparison set has area

\[ 2\pi R^2+4\pi h\rho -2\pi \rho ^2. \]

The maximum along this explicit radius family is $2\pi R^2+2\pi h^2$, attained at $\rho =h$. For fixed $R$ and $h\downarrow 0$, this excess is larger by an asymptotic factor $\log (R/h)$ than the thin catenoid's excess.

Proof

The two annuli contribute $2\pi (R^2-\rho ^2)$ and the cylinder, of height $2h$, contributes $4\pi h\rho $. The excess is $2\pi h^2-2\pi (\rho -h)^2$, which has the stated maximum because $h$ lies in the allowed interval. The ratio assertion follows from Theorem 10.6.3. At the joins this comparison set has corners; the displayed calculation alone neither supplies a smooth sweepout with controlled smoothing errors nor identifies a variational width. Those additional requirements belong to the deformation theorem, not this elementary area comparison. $\square$

Proposition 10.6.5 (The exact planar annulus capacity).C9FC5ED9776D Let $0<a<R$ and $\mathcal A=\{ x\in \mathbb R^2:a<|x|<R\} $. Among smooth functions on the closed annulus with boundary values zero on the inner circle and one on the outer circle, the unique minimizer of $\int _{\mathcal A}|\nabla u|^2$ is

\[ u_0(x)=\frac{\log (|x|/a)}{\log (R/a)},\qquad \int _{\mathcal A}|\nabla u_0|^2=\frac{2\pi }{\log (R/a)}. \]

For every such function $u$, the exact excess identity is

\[ \int _{\mathcal A}|\nabla u|^2 =\frac{2\pi }{\log (R/a)}+\int _{\mathcal A}|\nabla (u-u_0)|^2. \]
Proof

The radial Laplacian of $\log |x|$ is zero away from zero, so $u_0$ is harmonic. Its radial derivative is $1/(|x|\log (R/a))$; polar integration gives the stated energy. Write $u=u_0+v$, where $v$ vanishes on both boundary circles. Integration by parts gives $\int \nabla u_0\cdot \nabla v=0$. Expanding the square gives the identity. Equality forces $\nabla v=0$, and the boundary value then forces $v=0$. This is an exact minimization on a fixed planar annulus. Lemma 7.11.2 already uses the logarithmic profile to remove punctures; the present identity specifies the optimal constant and its equality case. $\square$

Proposition 10.6.6 (Quadratic sheet gain dominates a logarithmic error).4A98911260BF Let $\Sigma $ be a smooth closed embedded two-sided minimal surface in a smooth three-manifold, with chosen unit normal $\nu $. Suppose $\phi >0$ is smooth and its area second variation satisfies $Q(\phi ,\phi )=-q<0$. For small $t$, set

\[ \Sigma _ t=\{ \exp _ p(t\phi (p)\nu (p)):p\in \Sigma \} . \]

Then

\[ |\Sigma _ t|+|\Sigma _{-t}|=2|\Sigma |-qt^2+O(t^4). \]

If a collection of comparison surfaces $S_{t,s}$ additionally satisfies, uniformly in its parameter $s$,

\[ |S_{t,s}|\leq |\Sigma _ t|+|\Sigma _{-t}| +\frac{Ct^2}{\log (1/|t|)} \]

for some fixed $C$ and all sufficiently small $0<|t|<1$, then every surface in that collection has area at most $2|\Sigma |-(q/2)t^2$ once $t$ is small enough. The existence, topology and continuity of such a collection are additional hypotheses here, not conclusions of this estimate.

Proof

For small $t$, the normal exponential map is a tubular coordinate map on the compact embedded surface. The positivity of $\phi $ makes the two graphs disjoint when $t\ne 0$. Their area function $A(t)$ is smooth, with $A'(0)=0$ by minimality and $A''(0)=-q$ by Theorem 7.1.3. Taylor expansion through the cubic term gives $A(t)+A(-t)=2A(0)-qt^2+O(t^4)$, because the odd terms cancel. Choose $t$ small enough that the absolute remainder is at most $(q/4)t^2$ and $C/\log (1/|t|)\leq q/4$. Substituting into the assumed uniform comparison gives the result. This algebra explains the competition described in [KMN], p. 2; proving a uniformly controlled manifold deformation requires the separate geometric construction of its Section 2.2. $\square$
