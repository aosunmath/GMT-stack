16.18 Round surfaces and circular Willmore toriBC6F5B672660

BC6F5B672660

These calculations use the averaged energy $\mathcal W=\frac14\int H^2$ in Euclidean space and $\mathcal W_1=\int (1+H^2/4)$ in the unit three-sphere, where $H$ is the trace. The circular torus and its relation to the Clifford torus appear in [MNWill], p. 684; the calculations here supply their metrics, curvature and exact energies. The global positive-genus theorem remains the separate Theorem 10.1.2.

Proposition 16.18.1 (Geometry of a circular ring torus).737F11A69F2E For $R>r>0$, the map

\[ T(\theta ,\varphi )=\bigl ((R+r\cos \theta )\cos \varphi , (R+r\cos \theta )\sin \varphi ,r\sin \theta \bigr ), \qquad (\theta ,\varphi )\in (\mathbb R/2\pi \mathbb Z)^2, \]

is an embedded torus with metric $r^2d\theta ^2+(R+r\cos \theta )^2d\varphi ^2$ and area $4\pi ^2Rr$. With the outward normal $\nu =(\cos \theta \cos \varphi ,\cos \theta \sin \varphi ,\sin \theta )$, its principal curvatures are $1/r$ and $\cos \theta /(R+r\cos \theta )$.

Proof

Differentiate the parametrization. The two coordinate derivatives are orthogonal and have respective lengths $r$ and $R+r\cos \theta >0$, so it is an immersion with the stated metric. The positive distance to the rotation axis determines $\varphi $, while that distance minus $R$ together with height determines $(r\cos \theta ,r\sin \theta )$ and hence $\theta $. This proves injectivity and thus embeddedness by compactness. The area density is $r(R+r\cos \theta )$, whose double integral is $4\pi ^2Rr$. Differentiating the stated normal gives $\nu _\theta =T_\theta /r$ and $\nu _\varphi =\cos \theta \, T_\varphi /(R+r\cos \theta )$, proving the principal curvatures with $A=D\nu $. $\square$

Theorem 16.18.2 (The energy minimum within the circular ring family).CC8C3F901960 The preceding torus has

\[ \mathcal W(T)=\frac{\pi ^2R^2}{r\sqrt{R^2-r^2}}\geq 2\pi ^2, \]

with equality exactly when $R/r=\sqrt2$. Its energy tends to infinity as $R/r\downarrow 1$ or $R/r\to \infty $. This is the circular-family calculation motivating the conjecture recalled in [LY82], p. 270, and [MNWill], p. 684; it does not prove the inequality for arbitrary immersed tori.

Proof

Proposition 16.18.1 gives

\[ \mathcal W(T)=\frac{\pi }{2r}\int _0^{2\pi } \frac{(R+2r\cos \theta )^2}{R+r\cos \theta }\, d\theta . \]

Polynomial division makes the integrand $4r\cos \theta +R^2/(R+r\cos \theta )$. The cosine integrates to zero. The substitution $t=\tan (\theta /2)$ on $(-\pi ,\pi )$ gives

\[ \int _0^{2\pi }\frac{d\theta }{R+r\cos \theta } =2\int _{-\infty }^{\infty }\frac{dt}{(R+r)+(R-r)t^2} =\frac{2\pi }{\sqrt{R^2-r^2}}. \]

This proves the formula. Put $a=R/r>1$. The derivative of $a^2/\sqrt{a^2-1}$ is $a(a^2-2)/(a^2-1)^{3/2}$, negative below $\sqrt2$ and positive above it. Its value there is two, and the endpoint limits are infinite. Only the scale-free ratio matters, as conformal dilation invariance predicts. $\square$

Example 16.18.3 (Total Gauss curvature does not determine the torus energy).70E5321ABD3C Every circular ring torus above satisfies

\[ \int _ TK\, d\mu =0,\qquad \int _ T|K|\, d\mu =8\pi , \qquad \int _{\{ K>0\} }K\, d\mu =4\pi , \]

although its Willmore energy varies from $2\pi ^2$ to arbitrarily large values.

Proof

The principal curvatures give $K=\cos \theta /[r(R+r\cos \theta )]$. Multiplying by area leaves $\cos \theta \, d\theta d\varphi $. The integrals of $\cos \theta $, $|\cos \theta |$ and its positive part over a period are zero, four and two. Multiply each by $2\pi $. The energy variation follows from Theorem 16.18.2. Thus even fixing these total intrinsic-curvature quantities does not fix the bending energy. $\square$

Proposition 16.18.4 (Product tori in the unit three-sphere).F65993A50A9F For $a,b>0$ with $a^2+b^2=1$, the product torus

\[ F(\theta ,\varphi )=(a\cos \theta ,a\sin \theta ,b\cos \varphi ,b\sin \varphi ) \]

has area $4\pi ^2ab$, intrinsic Gauss curvature zero, and spherical principal curvatures $b/a$ and $-a/b$. Its spherical Willmore energy is $\pi ^2/(ab)\geq 2\pi ^2$, with equality exactly at $a=b=1/\sqrt2$. Within this family, only that Clifford torus is a Willmore critical surface.

Proof

The product metric is $a^2d\theta ^2+b^2d\varphi ^2$, flat with the claimed area. The field $\nu =(b\cos \theta ,b\sin \theta ,-a\cos \varphi ,-a\sin \varphi )$ is a unit vector tangent to $S^3$ and normal to the torus. Its derivatives in the two product directions give $b/a$ and $-a/b$. Thus $H=(b^2-a^2)/(ab)$ is constant, and

\[ 1+\frac{H^2}{4}=\frac{4a^2b^2+(b^2-a^2)^2}{4a^2b^2} =\frac1{4a^2b^2}. \]

Multiplication by area gives the energy. The inequality $ab\leq (a^2+b^2)/2=1/2$ proves its minimum. The trace-free curvature is nonzero everywhere, because the two principal curvatures have opposite signs. Hence the equation of Theorem 10.3.6 reduces to $H=0$, or $a=b$. The sphere here has radius one; it is different from the radius-two sphere containing the Clifford self-shrinker of Example 16.15.4. $\square$

Example 16.18.5 (Stereographic projection gives the circular minimizer).12ADEC1AC317 The stereographic projection from $(0,0,0,1)$ of the unit Clifford torus is the ring torus with major radius $\sqrt2$ and minor radius one. Both descriptions therefore have Willmore energy $2\pi ^2$.

Proof

Write a Clifford point as $(\cos \varphi ,\sin \varphi ,\cos \theta ,\sin \theta )/\sqrt2$. Its projected cylindrical coordinates are

\[ \rho =\frac1{\sqrt2-\sin \theta },\qquad z=\frac{\cos \theta }{\sqrt2-\sin \theta }. \]

The denominator is positive. Direct algebra gives $\rho ^2+z^2+1=2\sqrt2\rho $, or $(\rho -\sqrt2)^2+z^2=1$. Thus the image lies in the stated ring torus. Stereographic projection is a diffeomorphism off its pole, and the Clifford torus avoids the pole, so its restriction is an embedding. Its image in the ring torus is open by the inverse function theorem and closed by compactness; connectedness gives the entire ring torus. Proposition 16.18.4, Theorem 16.18.2 and the stereographic energy identity all give the same value. $\square$

Example 16.18.6 (All round latitudes have the same Willmore energy).46E3ED8F4006 For $0<\alpha <\pi $, the round latitude $F(\omega )=(\sin \alpha \, \omega ,\cos \alpha )$ in $S^3$, $\omega \in S^2$, has area $4\pi \sin ^2\alpha $, trace mean curvature $2\cot \alpha $ for a suitable normal, and $\mathcal W_1=4\pi $. Every latitude is Willmore critical, although only the equator is minimal.

Proof

The induced metric is $\sin ^2\alpha $ times the unit-sphere metric. The unit normal $\nu =(\cos \alpha \, \omega ,-\sin \alpha )$ differentiates to $A=\cot \alpha I$, proving the curvature and minimality assertion. The energy is $4\pi \sin ^2\alpha (1+\cot ^2\alpha )=4\pi $. Since $A^0=0$ and $H$ is constant, the Willmore equation vanishes. The ambient-curvature term one is necessary: the integral of $H^2/4$ alone is $4\pi \cos ^2\alpha $ and is not constant in this spherical family. $\square$

Example 16.18.7 (An affine plane becomes a punctured round sphere).E2F8CCE1C5C6 In a three-dimensional affine subspace identified with $\mathbb R^3$, invert at zero the plane $\{ x\cdot n=d\} $, where $|n|=1$ and $d\ne 0$. Its image is the sphere of center $n/(2d)$ and radius $1/(2|d|)$ with the origin removed. The plane has zero Willmore energy, while the punctured sphere has energy $4\pi $.

Proof

Writing $z=x/|x|^2$, the plane equation becomes $z\cdot n=d|z|^2$, equivalently $|z-n/(2d)|^2=1/(4d^2)$. Every nonzero point of this sphere inverts to a point of the plane, proving the image assertion. A plane is minimal; the round sphere's energy is $4\pi $, and removing one point does not change its integral. The original plane is noncompact, so invariance for a closed surface with a pole off its image does not apply in that direction. Inverting the completed sphere at its origin instead fits Proposition 10.4.5: it loses exactly $4\pi $ and becomes the plane. $\square$

Proposition 16.18.8 (The full Willmore equation on a ring torus).3F8B80E15359 For the circular ring torus of Proposition 16.18.1, write $s=R+r\cos \theta $. Then

\[ \Delta H+H|A^0|^2=\frac{R(R^2-2r^2)}{2r^3s^3}. \]

Consequently the ratio $R/r=\sqrt2$ gives a critical point under all smooth variations, not merely under variations within the ring family.

Proof

The trace is $H=1/r+\cos \theta /s$, and its derivative is $H_\theta =-R\sin \theta /s^2$. For a function of $\theta $ alone, the product metric gives

\[ \Delta H=\frac1{r^2}H_{\theta \theta }+\frac{s_\theta }{r^2s}H_\theta =-\frac{R\cos \theta }{r^2s^2}-\frac{R\sin ^2\theta }{rs^3}. \]

Also $|A^0|^2=(1/r-\cos \theta /s)^2/2=R^2/(2r^2s^2)$. Bringing the sum to the common denominator $2r^3s^3$ leaves

\[ R\bigl [-2r\cos \theta \, s-2r^2\sin ^2\theta +R(s+r\cos \theta )\bigr ] =R(R^2-2r^2). \]

The denominator is everywhere positive. The first-variation theorem therefore gives the assertion, including the converse within this circular family. A minimum found in a restricted family would not by itself justify the full criticality conclusion; the displayed equation does. $\square$

Proposition 16.18.9 (Second variation at a round Euclidean sphere).24AEED595178 For the round sphere $S_ R^2\subset \mathbb R^3$, a smooth variation with initial normal speed $f$ has

\[ \left.\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\right|_0\mathcal W(F_ t) =\frac12\int _{S_ R^2}(\Delta f)^2d\mu -\frac1{R^2}\int _{S_ R^2}|\nabla f|^2d\mu =\int _{S_ R^2}\left|\nabla ^2f-\frac{\Delta f}{2}g\right|^2d\mu . \]

In particular every such second variation is nonnegative, independently of the second-order choice of the variation.

Proof

On the sphere, $H=2/R$ is constant and $A^0=0$. Differentiate the integrand of Theorem 10.3.6. Since the original Euler–Lagrange expression is zero, derivatives of speed and area do not contribute. The derivative of $H|A^0|^2$ is zero. The derivative of the Laplacian acting on the original constant $H$ is also zero, because every Laplacian annihilates a constant. Thus only $\Delta \dot H$ remains. The Euclidean linearization gives $\dot H=-\Delta f-2f/R^2$. This yields

\[ \ddot{\mathcal W}=\frac12\int f\Delta (\Delta +2/R^2)f, \]

which is the first formula after integration by parts.

For completeness, commuting one covariant derivative of $\nabla f$ gives $\operatorname {div}(\nabla ^2f)=\nabla \Delta f+\operatorname {Ric}(\nabla f,\cdot )$. On this two-sphere $\operatorname {Ric}=R^{-2}g$. Integrating the divergence identity against $\nabla f$ gives

\[ \int |\nabla ^2f|^2=\int (\Delta f)^2-\frac1{R^2}\int |\nabla f|^2. \]

Subtracting half the squared trace produces exactly the preceding Hessian formula. Nonnegativity is now the integral of a squared norm; no spectral decomposition is needed. $\square$

Proposition 16.18.10 (Only dilations and translations lie in the spherical kernel).FF20FCA045C5 The second variation in Proposition 16.18.9 vanishes exactly for functions $f=c+a\cdot X$, with $c\in \mathbb R$ and $a\in \mathbb R^3$. The constant mode is the normal speed of a change of radius; the linear modes are normal translation speeds up to a fixed factor. The normal kernel is therefore four-dimensional.

Proof

Vanishing of the square gives $\nabla ^2f=(\Delta f/2)g$. Take its divergence and use the commutation identity from the preceding proof. It follows that $\nabla (\Delta f+2f/R^2)=0$. Averaging this constant expression over the sphere shows that $u=f-\overline f$ satisfies $\Delta u=-2u/R^2$ and hence $\nabla ^2u=-ug/R^2$.

Regard $\nabla u$ as an ambient tangent vector. The Euclidean derivative of $Z=\nabla u+(u/R^2)X$ in a tangent direction $V$ is zero: its tangential terms are $-uV/R^2+uV/R^2$, and its normal terms are $-(V u)X/R^2+(V u)X/R^2$, because the sphere's vector-valued second fundamental form is $B(V,W)=-g(V,W)X/R^2$. Connectedness makes $Z=a$ one constant ambient vector. Dotting with $X$ gives $u=a\cdot X$. Conversely every such linear function has Hessian $-ug/R^2$, and constants have zero Hessian, so they lie in the kernel. A translation by a vector $b$ has normal speed $b\cdot X/R$, which spans the same three-dimensional space. These are infinitesimal kernel statements and do not assert convergence of a geometric flow. $\square$
