16.12 A one-dimensional dust without tangent lines7B305BAC5FA6

7B305BAC5FA6

The four-corner Cantor set is the example following Theorem 3.17 in [Mor16], fifth edition, pp. 36–37. We prove finite positive length, a quantitative obstruction to tangent lines, and pure unrectifiability. The last step explicitly uses the rectifiable tangent theorem, Theorem 3.1.4; the elementary estimates do not claim the source's exact value of the Hausdorff measure.

Definition 16.12.1 (The four-corner set and cylinder measure).F9CBD348B9C0 Let

\[ C_4=\left\{ \sum _{j\ge 1}3\varepsilon _ j4^{-j}:\varepsilon _ j\in \{ 0,1\} \right\} ,\qquad K=C_4\times C_4. \]

At level $k$, the set $K$ lies in $4^ k$ disjoint corner squares of side $4^{-k}$. A cylinder is the intersection of $K$ with one such square. Let $\mu $ be the probability measure assigning mass $4^{-k}$ to each level-$k$ cylinder. Equivalently, push forward Lebesgue probability on $[0,1]^2$ by the two binary-to-base-four digit maps; the dyadic endpoint choices affect a null set only.

Proposition 16.12.2 (Linear ball bounds and finite positive length).51869A534A53 For $x\in K$ and $0<r\le \sqrt2$,

\[ \frac{r}{8\sqrt2}\le \mu (B_ r(x))\le 100r. \]

The upper bound also holds for balls centered outside $K$. Consequently $0<\mathcal H^1(K)<\infty $ and $\dim _{\rm H}K=1$; more precisely $1/100\le \mathcal H^1(K)\le \sqrt2$.

Proof

For $r<1$, choose $k$ with $\ell =4^{-k}\le r<4\ell $. The lower-left corners of level-$k$ squares lie on the grid $\ell \mathbb Z^2$. A square meeting a ball of radius $r$ has each lower-left coordinate in an interval of length at most $2r+\ell <9\ell $. There are at most ten grid choices per coordinate and hence at most one hundred such squares. Each has $\mu $ mass $\ell $, so $\mu (B_ r)\le 100\ell \le 100r$. For $r\ge 1$ the bound follows from total mass one.

For the lower bound choose the least $k$ with $\sqrt2\, 4^{-k}\le r/2$. The level-$k$ cylinder containing $x$ lies entirely in $B_ r(x)$, and minimality gives $4^{-k}>r/(8\sqrt2)$. Thus its mass proves the lower estimate. The same cylinder masses show that every point has zero mass.

The level-$k$ squares cover $K$ with total diameter $4^ k\sqrt2\, 4^{-k}=\sqrt2$, proving the Hausdorff upper bound. For any sufficiently small countable cover $K\subset \bigcup U_ i$, discard sets missing $K$ and choose $x_ i\in U_ i\cap K$. Then $U_ i\cap K\subset \overline B_{\operatorname {diam}U_ i}(x_ i)$. The ball estimate, obtained for closed balls by enlarging the radius and taking a limit, gives $1\le 100\sum _ i\operatorname {diam}U_ i$. Infimizing and shrinking the cover diameters gives the lower bound. A set of finite positive $\mathcal H^1$ measure has Hausdorff dimension one by the comparison of diameter powers in the Hausdorff definition. $\square$

Proposition 16.12.3 (The cylinder measure is normalized Hausdorff measure).8A4F1392293E The identity $\mu =\mathcal H^1\llcorner K/\mathcal H^1(K)$ holds. In particular a level-$k$ cylinder has $\mathcal H^1$ measure $4^{-k}\mathcal H^1(K)$.

Proof

Every level-$k$ cylinder is a homothetic copy of $K$ with ratio $4^{-k}$. Hausdorff one-measure scales by that ratio, directly from its definition by diameter covers. Thus normalized Hausdorff measure and $\mu $ agree on every cylinder. Finite unions of cylinders form an algebra, since the partitions refine each other, and this algebra generates the Borel sets of $K$: cylinder diameters tend to zero and every relative neighborhood contains a cylinder around each of its points. Uniqueness of finite measures on a generating algebra gives equality. All measures here are Borel measures on the compact set $K$. $\square$

Lemma 16.12.4 (Differentiation along the Cantor cylinders).2FE1FC9C5147 For $f\in L^1(\mu )$, let $Q_ k(x)$ be the level-$k$ cylinder containing $x$, and put

\[ A_ kf(x)=\frac1{\mu (Q_ k(x))}\int _{Q_ k(x)}f\, d\mu . \]

Then $A_ kf(x)\to f(x)$ for $\mu $-almost every $x$. In particular, for a $\mu $-measurable subset $E\subset K$, at almost every point of $E$ the ratio $\mu (Q_ k(x)\setminus E)/\mu (Q_ k(x))$ tends to zero.

Proof

The cylinder maximal function $Mg=\sup _{k\ge 0}A_ k|g|$ satisfies

\[ \mu \{ Mg>t\} \le t^{-1}\| g\| _{L^1(\mu )}. \]

For a finite range of levels, select the first cylinder in each nested chain whose average exceeds $t$. These stopping cylinders are disjoint and cover the set in question; summing $t\mu (Q)<\int _ Q|g|$ proves the inequality. Increasing the range gives the full estimate by continuity from below.

For a continuous function on $K$, the averages converge uniformly to the function, because cylinder diameters tend uniformly to zero. Continuous restrictions of compactly supported ambient functions are dense in $L^1(\mu )$ by Theorem 2.3.8. Given such an approximation $h$ to $f$, the limit superior of $|A_ kf-f|$ is at most $M(f-h)+|f-h|$. The maximal inequality and Markov's inequality bound the measure where this exceeds $2t$ by $2t^{-1}\| f-h\| _1$. Let the approximation error tend to zero and then take countably many positive rational $t$. This proves almost-everywhere convergence. Apply it to $f=\chi _ E$ for the density assertion. $\square$

Lemma 16.12.5 (Every line misses a substantial corner).8A401CAA49F4 Let $Q$ be a level-$k$ square of side $\ell $. For every affine line $L\subset \mathbb R^2$, one of the four child squares $Q'$ satisfies

\[ \operatorname {dist}(y,L)\ge \frac{\ell }{8\sqrt2}\quad \hbox{for all }y\in Q'. \]

Its cylinder has $\mathcal H^1$ measure $\ell \mathcal H^1(K)/4$.

Proof

The four child centers form a square of side $3\ell /4$. Project onto a unit normal of $L$. The range of their projections is at least $3\ell /(4\sqrt2)$, so at least one center has distance at least $3\ell /(8\sqrt2)$ from $L$. Every point of its child square is within the half diagonal $\ell /(4\sqrt2)$ of that center. The triangle inequality gives the claimed distance. The measure is the cylinder formula of Proposition 16.12.3. $\square$

Proposition 16.12.6 (No approximate tangent line at any point).E41DED080922 For every $x\in K$ and every line $P$ through the origin, $P$ is not an approximate tangent to $K$ at $x$ in Definition 3.1.3. More quantitatively, the measure outside a fixed narrow cone about $x+P$ is bounded below by a positive multiple of radius along the cylinder scales.

Proof

Take the parent square of side $\ell =4^{-k}$ containing $x$. Lemma 16.12.5 supplies a child cylinder of mass $\ell \mathcal H^1(K)/4$ at distance at least $\ell /(8\sqrt2)$ from $x+P$. All its points are within $\sqrt2\ell $ of $x$, so their angle from $P$ has sine at least $1/16$. The mass in $B_{2\sqrt2\ell }(x)$ outside the cone of sine aperture $1/32$ is therefore at least $\ell \mathcal H^1(K)/4$.

This also directly contradicts the measure-limit tangent definition. After scaling by $2\sqrt2\ell $, the child lies in the fixed set $\{ |y|\le 1/2,\operatorname {dist}(y,P)\ge 1/32\} $. Choose a continuous compactly supported nonnegative test equal to one on this set and zero on $P$. Its integral against the rescaled measures has a uniform positive lower bound, whereas its integral on $P$ is zero. Hence the required convergence cannot hold. $\square$

Theorem 16.12.7 (Pure unrectifiability of the four-corner set).30075247956C For every countably one-rectifiable set $A\subset \mathbb R^2$, $\mathcal H^1(A\cap K)=0$. This is the example in [Mor16], pp. 36–37. The proof below uses the external rectifiable tangent theorem, Theorem 3.1.4, and the locally proved cylinder differentiation lemma.

Proof

It suffices to treat the image of a Lipschitz map on a compact interval, since countably many such images cover any countably rectifiable set up to a null set. Such an image is compact and has finite length, by the elementary diameter-cover bound for a Lipschitz map. Let $E$ be its intersection with $K$ and suppose $\mathcal H^1(E)>0$. Then $E$ is rectifiable, measurable and finite in measure, so Theorem 3.1.4 gives an approximate tangent at almost every point of $E$. By Lemma 16.12.4 and Proposition 16.12.3, choose such a point $x$ where

\[ \mathcal H^1((K\cap Q_ k(x))\setminus E)=o(4^{-k}). \]

The child corner furnished by Lemma 16.12.5 for its tangent line contains $\ell \mathcal H^1(K)/4$ of $K$ measure, of which only $o(\ell )$ is missing from $E$. The same continuous test used in Proposition 16.12.6 therefore has a uniformly positive integral against the rescaled measures of $E$. This contradicts its approximate tangent. Thus the intersection with every compact Lipschitz curve has zero length; countable union and the null exceptional set prove the assertion. $\square$

Example 16.12.8 (An exceptional projection contains an interval).2508D7A1F8C5 Both coordinate projections of $K$ have Lebesgue measure zero. In contrast, the orthogonal projection onto the direction $(2,1)/\sqrt5$ is the interval $[0,3/\sqrt5]$.

Proof

A coordinate projection equals $C_4$, whose level-$k$ cover consists of $2^ k$ intervals of length $4^{-k}$; their total length tends to zero. For points of $K$,

\[ 2x+y=3\sum _{j\ge 1}(2\varepsilon _ j+\delta _ j)4^{-j}. \]

The digit $2\varepsilon _ j+\delta _ j$ takes each value $0,1,2,3$, independently at every level. These are exactly the base-four expansions of the interval $[0,1]$, including the endpoint expansion with every digit three. Hence $2x+y$ ranges over $[0,3]$. Division by $\sqrt5$ gives the orthogonal projection coordinate. This is a statement about particular directions; it does not purport to prove the almost-everywhere projection theorem for purely unrectifiable sets. $\square$
