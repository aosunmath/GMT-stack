16.7 The equatorial disk and critical catenoidB94F09743301

B94F09743301

These examples connect the catenoid calculation in Example 16.2.2 to the free-boundary geometry of Section 7.17. The critical catenoid and its Steklov spectrum are discussed in [FS11], Section 3, pp. 6–9. We derive the boundary condition and scale directly, and distinguish a negative variation from a calculation of the full Morse index.

Example 16.7.1 (The equatorial ball).8918496434FC The flat unit ball $D^ n=\overline B^{n+1}\cap \{ x_{n+1}=0\} $ is free-boundary minimal. It has volume $\omega _ n$, boundary volume $n\omega _ n$, and $A=0$. A constant normal speed has $Q(1,1)=-n\omega _ n$. Each coordinate function $x_ i$, $1\leq i\leq n$, is a Jacobi field for the free-boundary second variation, meaning $Q(x_ i,f)=0$ for every smooth $f$.

Proof

Flatness gives minimality, and the outward conormal of the unit ball is its position vector. The volume formulas and Proposition 7.17.9 give the negative variation. Since $\Delta x_ i=0$ and $\partial _\eta x_ i=x_ i$, integration by parts gives $Q(x_ i,f)=\int _ D\langle \nabla x_ i,\nabla f\rangle -\int _{\partial D}x_ if=0$. These fields arise from rotations that tilt the equatorial plane; the constant speed does not arise from such a symmetry. $\square$

Proposition 16.7.2 (The Steklov spectrum of the flat disk).6EFFF85D2747 For the unit disk $D^2$ with its flat metric, the Steklov eigenvalues are $0,1,2,\ldots $. The zero eigenspace is the constants; for integer $k\geq 1$, the eigenvalue $k$ has basis $r^ k\cos (k\theta )$ and $r^ k\sin (k\theta )$.

Proof

A smooth harmonic function on the closed disk has the harmonic Fourier expansion

\[ u(r,\theta )=a_0+\sum _{k\geq 1}r^ k(a_ k\cos k\theta +b_ k\sin k\theta ). \]

This follows by taking Fourier coefficients in the harmonic equation: the radial ODE has solutions $r^{\pm k}$, of which only $r^ k$ is smooth at the origin; its zero mode is constant. The boundary condition $u_ r=\sigma u$ at $r=1$ makes $(k-\sigma )a_ k=(k-\sigma )b_ k=0$ and $\sigma a_0=0$. Fourier uniqueness proves the assertion, while each displayed function directly realizes its eigenvalue. $\square$

Proposition 16.7.3 (Index one and nullity two for the equatorial disk).492DCAF81B02 The equatorial unit disk in $B^3$ has free-boundary Morse index one and nullity two. Here the index is the largest dimension of a subspace on which $Q$ is negative definite, and nullity is the dimension of the bilinear kernel $\{ f:Q(f,g)=0\text{ for every }g\} $.

Proof

If the boundary trace of $f$ is $a_0+\sum _{k\geq 1}(a_ k\cos k\theta +b_ k\sin k\theta )$, let $h$ be its harmonic extension. Green's identity gives $\int _ D|\nabla f|^2=\int _ D|\nabla h|^2+\int _ D|\nabla (f-h)|^2$. Orthogonality of the trigonometric modes yields

\[ Q(f,f)=\int _ D|\nabla (f-h)|^2-2\pi a_0^2 +\pi \sum _{k\geq 1}(k-1)(a_ k^2+b_ k^2). \]

The codimension-one subspace of functions with zero boundary mean is nonnegative for $Q$, so a negative-definite subspace has dimension at most one. The constant function gives a negative direction, proving index one. If $Q(f,g)=0$ for all $g$, interior tests imply $\Delta f=0$ and the boundary terms give $\partial _\eta f=f$. Proposition 16.7.2 identifies its solutions as the span of $x_1,x_2$. Conversely those functions are in the kernel by Example 16.7.1. The zero cone $Q(f,f)=0$ is not being identified with this bilinear kernel. $\square$

Lemma 16.7.4 (The critical catenoid parameter).9BF4DE659360 There is a unique $t_0>0$ such that $t_0\tanh t_0=1$, and $t_0>1$. Put

\[ a=\frac1{t_0\cosh t_0}. \]

Then $a=(\cosh ^2t_0+t_0^2)^{-1/2}$, $a\cosh t_0=1/t_0$, and $at_0=\operatorname {sech}t_0$.

Proof

The function $t\tanh t$ starts at zero, has derivative $\tanh t+t\operatorname {sech}^2t>0$ for $t>0$, and tends to infinity. Its value at $1$ is less than one. The root exists uniquely and exceeds one. From $\tanh t_0=1/t_0$ and $\operatorname {sech}^2t_0=1-1/t_0^2$, one gets $\cosh ^2t_0+t_0^2=t_0^2\cosh ^2t_0$, proving the scale identities. This is the centered symmetric case of [FS11], Theorem 3.1. $\square$

Example 16.7.5 (A free-boundary catenoidal annulus).DD8FCA5146D1 With $a,t_0$ as above, the immersion

\[ F(t,\theta )=a(\cosh t\cos \theta ,\cosh t\sin \theta ,t), \qquad -t_0\leq t\leq t_0,\quad \theta \in \mathbb R/(2\pi \mathbb Z), \]

is a smooth embedded free-boundary minimal annulus in the unit ball. Its boundary circles have radius $1/t_0$ and heights $\pm \operatorname {sech}t_0$. Its metric is $a^2\cosh ^2t\, (dt^2+d\theta ^2)$.

Proof

Example 16.2.2 gives the metric, minimality, and embeddedness. Since $|F|^2=a^2(\cosh ^2t+t^2)$ is strictly increasing in $|t|>0$, the scale identity gives $|F|=1$ exactly at $t=\pm t_0$ and $|F|<1$ in the interior. At the upper circle the unit conormal is

\[ \frac{F_ t}{|F_ t|}=(\tanh t_0\cos \theta ,\tanh t_0\sin \theta ,\operatorname {sech}t_0)=F(t_0,\theta ). \]

At the lower circle the outward conormal is $-F_ t/|F_ t|$, which similarly equals $F(-t_0,\theta )$. The scale identities give the stated circle radii and heights. This establishes the free-boundary condition directly. $\square$

Proposition 16.7.6 (Area, boundary length, and an unstable direction).521D410B62DA For the critical catenoid $C$,

\[ |C|=\frac{2\pi }{t_0},\qquad |\partial C|=\frac{4\pi }{t_0},\qquad \int _ C|A|^2=\frac{8\pi }{t_0},\qquad Q(1,1)=-\frac{12\pi }{t_0}. \]

These computations exhibit a negative variation but do not determine the full Morse index.

Proof

The area integral is $2\pi a^2\int _{-t_0}^{t_0}\cosh ^2t\, dt=2\pi a^2(t_0+\sinh t_0\cosh t_0)=2\pi /t_0$. For the simplification, use $a^2=1/(t_0^2\cosh ^2t_0)$ and $\sinh t_0=\cosh t_0/t_0$. The two boundary circles give length $4\pi /t_0$, also checking $|\partial C|=2|C|$. By Proposition 16.2.4, $|A|^2=2/(a^2\cosh ^4t)$. Multiplication by the area element gives $2\operatorname {sech}^2t\, dt\, d\theta $, whose integral is $8\pi \tanh t_0=8\pi /t_0$. Substitute into the normal quadratic form. $\square$

Proposition 16.7.7 (First Steklov eigenspace of the critical catenoid).0D411F8E126C The critical catenoid has first positive Steklov eigenvalue $1$, of multiplicity three, with eigenspace spanned by its three ambient coordinate functions. In particular $\sigma _1|\partial C|=4\pi /t_0$.

Proof

In dimension two, the conformal factor in the metric cancels from the harmonic equation. Thus it is $u_{tt}+u_{\theta \theta }=0$. At either boundary the unit outward derivative is $\pm t_0\partial _ t$, since $a\cosh t_0=1/t_0$. The angular zero mode is affine in $t$; its constant solution has eigenvalue zero, and its odd solution $t$ has eigenvalue one. For integer $k\geq 1$, the even modes $\cosh (kt)\cos (k\theta )$ and $\cosh (kt)\sin (k\theta )$ have eigenvalue

\[ \sigma _ k^{\rm even}=t_0k\tanh (kt_0), \]

while the analogous odd modes with $\sinh (kt)$ have eigenvalue $\sigma _ k^{\rm odd}=t_0k\coth (kt_0)$. Fourier decomposition and reflection into even and odd parts exhaust the smooth harmonic eigenfunctions. The function $s\mapsto s\tanh (st_0)$ is strictly increasing for $s>0$. Hence its smallest integer-mode value is $\sigma _1^{\rm even}=t_0\tanh t_0=1$; all $k\geq 2$ even values exceed one. Every odd value is greater than $t_0k>1$. Together with the zero-angular-mode function $t$, this proves precisely three eigenfunctions at one, which are multiples of $F_1,F_2,F_3$. This specializes the separation of variables in [FS11], pp. 7–9; it makes no claim about the sharp bound among all annular metrics. $\square$
